# Voice and technique

How to develop one writing style across forty pieces of content, and the
techniques that make each one interesting.

## How you actually develop a house style

Not by writing a style guide first. A guide written before the content
describes an imaginary voice, and the first real piece breaks it.

The order that works is the reverse:

1. **Write three specimens at the far ends of the range.** An easy one, a hard
   one, and an awkward one. If a voice holds at both extremes it will hold in
   the middle. Picking three adjacent easy topics teaches you nothing, because
   anything sounds consistent across three easy topics.
2. **Read them together and mark what they share.** Not what you meant to do.
   What they actually do: sentence length, where the term first appears, who
   the subject of the sentence is, whether questions are asked or answered.
3. **Write the rules from the marks.** Every rule must name something visible in
   the specimens. A rule you cannot point at is a preference, and it will be
   ignored under deadline.
4. **Freeze the specimens as the reference.** New content is written *against
   the specimen*, not against the rules. People match a sample far more reliably
   than they follow a list.
5. **Make the checkable rules checkable.** This project gates its work. Word
   budgets, term-before-example ordering, and forbidden constructions can all be
   tested in the content compiler. Rules that cannot be tested stay advisory and
   should be few.

The three specimens for this project are at the bottom of this document. They
cover lesson 3 (early, mechanical), lesson 14 (middle, subtle), and lesson 27
(late, ambiguous).

## The one rule everything else hangs on

`../../AGENTS.md` already states it: **plain language first, the term second.**
Introduce a name for something the reader already understands, never as the
thing to be understood.

Three sources found in this research say the same thing independently:

- **3Blue1Brown:** definitions should be an ending point, not a starting point.
  The best pedagogical order is often not the correct logical order.
- **Nicky Case:** start on the ground, give the reader a concrete experience
  first. The failure mode is answering a question the reader has not yet become
  curious about.
- **Myhill's LEAD:** explain with examples rather than long explanations.

So the shape of every piece is fixed by that one rule, and it is not the
five-part lesson:

```text
a real sentence  →  something you do to it  →  what you noticed  →  its name
```

The name arrives fourth. If it arrives first, the piece is wrong regardless of
how well it is written.

## Ten techniques

These are the moves available. Each one is a way of making a grammatical fact
*happen* on screen rather than be asserted. Every piece uses one as its spine
and may borrow a second.

### 1. The failed test

Open with a definition the reader already believes, then break it.

> A noun is a person, place, or thing. So which is *justice*?

Good for anything school grammar taught badly: noun, verb, subject, direct
object. Use once per stage at most. It is a strong move and it goes stale.

### 2. The minimal pair

Two sentences, one difference. The reader says what changed before anything is
named. This is the noticing technique from second-language research, and it is
the cheapest thing on this list to author.

> The soup tasted salty.
> The soup tasted the salt.

### 3. The substitution

Swap a run of words for one word and see if the sentence survives. The noun
phrase test, and the clearest single demonstration the app can perform.

### 4. The deletion

Take something out and see what breaks. The only honest way to show an
obligatory adverbial, and the only way to show that a modifier is optional.

### 5. The rearrangement

Move it and see if it survives the trip. This is the movement constituency test,
and it also carries word order, questions, and the passive.

### 6. The paraphrase pivot

Same words, two structures, two meanings. Move one line in the diagram and
change the sentence's meaning without touching a word. The app's signature move
and the payoff of the whole course.

### 7. The wrong-but-reasonable

Name the mistake a smart reader would make, and name it *before* they make it.

> "Tasted what? Salty." That is the right question and the wrong answer.

The EFL literature has already found which errors these are. SVOC is documented
as the hardest pattern because the object and complement sit side by side and
learners read the complement as a second object. Write that error down before
writing the explanation.

### 8. The count

Say how many there are. Finite sets are a relief.

> There are six. That is the whole list, and every English clause you will ever
> read uses one of them.

Works for the verb types, the word classes, the clause patterns. Never invent a
count to get the effect.

### 9. The borrowed frame

One metaphor, used once, then dropped. The Form-Function Method's occupation
frame is the best available for form against function: the form *man* performs
the functions *father* and *teacher*; the function *teacher* is performed by the
forms *man* and *woman*. Two sentences, and the whole distinction lands.

Rule: a frame is allowed to explain, never to decide. The moment a reader
answers a grammar question from the metaphor instead of from the sentence, the
frame has to go.

### 10. The real sentence

Never invent an example when a real one exists. Morenberg's book runs on
sentences from books, magazines, and newspapers; Myhill's LEAD names authentic
texts as a principle; Englicious draws its examples from language corpora.

Invented examples are permitted only for a minimal pair, where the point is that
exactly one thing differs, and the pair must be marked as constructed.

## Technique by concept

Each concept has a move that fits it. Pairing them well is most of what makes
the writing interesting, because the technique *is* the content when it is
chosen correctly.

| Concept | Spine technique | Why it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subject and predicate | Rearrangement | Position and agreement are visible; the doer is not |
| Main verb | Transformation through tense | The verb identifies itself by being the word that must change |
| Noun phrase | Substitution | One word replaces the run, or it does not |
| Head | Deletion | Strip modifiers until one word is left standing |
| Determiner | The count | Only one article per phrase, and it comes first |
| Pronoun | Substitution | It is the substitution test's own answer |
| The six verb types | The count | A closed set that predicts everything after it |
| Direct object | The wrong-but-reasonable | "Verb what?" works until it does not |
| Subject complement | Minimal pair | *tasted salty* against *tasted the salt* |
| Two objects | Rearrangement | *gave her the book* / *gave the book to her* |
| Object complement | The wrong-but-reasonable | The documented confusion is a second object |
| Obligatory adverbial | Deletion | Removal is the entire distinction |
| Adjectives and adverbs | Minimal pair | The "very" test does not separate them; position does |
| Prepositional phrase | Substitution | It moves and survives as one piece |
| Form against function | The borrowed frame | The occupation metaphor, once |
| Postmodifiers | Deletion | What is left is the head |
| Appositive | Substitution | Either half can stand alone in the slot |
| Auxiliaries | Transformation | Tense moves off the main verb and onto the helper |
| Particles | Rearrangement | The object may move past a particle, never into a PP |
| Coordination | The count | Both sides must be the same kind of thing |
| Attachment | Paraphrase pivot | The whole point of the app |
| Clause types | Substitution | A clause fills a slot a phrase could have filled |
| Passive | Rearrangement | The participants stay; their roles swap |
| Ambiguity | Paraphrase pivot | Two readings, both correct, different meanings |

Two rules govern the table. **No technique twice in a row**, because the reader
starts predicting the move instead of doing it. And **the paraphrase pivot is
rationed**: it is the closing move of the course, so spending it early costs the
ending.

## Voice rules

Written from the specimens. Every one names something you can point at.

**Ordering**

- The technical term appears after the reader has done the thing it names.
- The first sentence of a piece contains no metalanguage. It is a real sentence,
  or a question about one.
- The piece closes on a sentence that breaks what it just established.

**Person and tense**

- Second person for anything the reader does. "Take it out and read what is
  left."
- No "we." The app is not a person standing next to the reader.
- Present tense throughout, including for the example sentences' behaviour.

**Sentences**

- One clause carries one claim. If a sentence has two claims, it becomes two
  sentences.
- Under twenty words for anything on the always-visible layer.
- No sentence describes what the diagram is already showing. The prose names the
  test and the consequence; the diagram shows the change.

**Words**

- The plain word beats the precise word when both are true. "Take it out" beats
  "delete the constituent."
- No hedging on grammatical facts. Hedge only on genuinely open analyses, and
  then say why it is open.
- No praise. "Correct" is information; "Great job!" is noise, and the app's own
  motion vocabulary already bans the visual version of it.

**Honesty**

- A test that will be revised later says so, and names where.
- When two readings are both right, neither is described as the answer.
- A claim the reader cannot check on the sentence in front of them does not
  belong in the required layer.

## Three specimens

These are the reference. New content is matched against these, not against the
rules above.

### Specimen A — lesson 3, "Find the main verb"

Early, mechanical, spine technique: transformation.

> **Which word is the verb?**
>
> *The engine stalled on the hill.*
>
> Say it as if it is happening now.
>
> > The engine **stalls** on the hill.
>
> One word changed. *Engine* did not. *Hill* did not. *On* did not.
>
> The word that changes when you move a sentence through time is the verb. That
> is the whole test. Unlike "a verb is a doing word," it does not ask you to
> decide whether stalling counts as doing something.
>
> Try it where nothing is happening at all: *The house stood empty.* Move it to
> now and *stood* becomes *stands*. Nobody did anything, and the test still
> found the verb.
>
> **Now this one:** *The engine on the hill stalled.* Which words did you have to
> step over to get there?

What it demonstrates: the term arrives in sentence five, the reader performs the
transformation before reading what it proves, the failed notional definition
appears as one clause and is not laboured, and the close is a genuine
counterexample that opens noun phrases.

### Specimen B — lesson 14, "When an adverbial is required"

Middle, subtle, spine technique: deletion.

> **Some phrases cannot leave**
>
> *She put the book on the shelf.*
>
> Take *on the shelf* out.
>
> > She put the book.
>
> That is not a sentence any more. Now do the same thing here.
>
> *She read the book on the train.*
>
> > She read the book.
>
> Fine. Nothing broke.
>
> The two phrases are built the same way. Both start with a preposition, both
> name a place, both sit at the end. The difference is not in the phrase at all.
> It is in the verb. *Put* demands a place. *Read* does not.
>
> So the test is removal. Take the phrase out. If the sentence survives, the
> phrase was optional. If the sentence collapses, the verb was holding on to it.
>
> **Now this one:** *The keys are on the table.* Take the phrase out and you get
> *The keys are.*

What it demonstrates: two parallel deletions before any claim is made, the
misdirection handled explicitly (the phrases look identical, so say so), the
rule stated only after both results are visible, and a close that hands lesson
11's unfinished business back to the reader.

### Specimen C — lesson 27, "Attachment changes meaning"

Late, ambiguous, spine technique: paraphrase pivot.

> **Who had the telescope?**
>
> *I saw the man with the telescope.*
>
> That sentence has two meanings, and you have probably only read one of them.
>
> Attach *with the telescope* to *saw*, and the telescope is what you looked
> through. Attach it to *the man*, and the telescope is what he was carrying.
>
> Same words. Same order. One line moved.
>
> Nothing inside the sentence decides it. Not the grammar, not the punctuation,
> not the word order. Only the situation decides, and the sentence does not
> contain the situation.
>
> This is not a flaw in English and it is not a trick question. Every sentence
> you have built so far was one attachment away from meaning something else.
> This is the first one where both readings are worth having.
>
> **Build either one.** You will not be marked on which you chose. You will be
> marked on whether your drawing and your paraphrase agree.

What it demonstrates: the reader is told a thing about their own reading before
being taught anything, the pivot is stated in two symmetrical sentences, the
"not a trick" line does the emotional work the assessment stance requires, and
the close states the grading rule plainly instead of reassuring.

## What to do with this

1. Treat the three specimens as fixed. Edit them until they are right, then stop.
2. Write two more for unit types the specimens do not cover: a reference entry
   and a diagnosis. Those are different jobs and will need their own reference.
3. Turn the checkable voice rules into compiler checks, starting with the two
   that are mechanical: the first sentence contains no metalanguage, and the
   term does not appear before its first example.
4. Fill the technique table's remaining rows as content is authored, and enforce
   the no-repeat rule in the compiler rather than by memory.
