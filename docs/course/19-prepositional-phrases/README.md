## What the lesson decides

Nothing new enters the label menu here. `P`, `PP`, and `complement` arrived in
lesson 14. This lesson should make their **structure** visible: a preposition
heads a phrase, and its complement can contain another phrase rather than being
only one noun phrase after one word.

## Central generalization

A **prepositional phrase** is a phrase headed by a preposition. A preposition
commonly has a noun-phrase complement, but it can also take another
prepositional phrase or another kind of complement; the whole phrase can then
occupy a position in a clause or inside another phrase.

Thus _under that table_ is a PP headed by _under_, with _that table_ as its
complement. In _out of the wood_, the course analyses _out_ as the head of the
larger PP and _of the wood_ as its PP complement. The phrase is not defined by
word count, place meaning, or a particular job in a sentence.

This is the course's analysis, not the only classroom convention. Many school
descriptions call _out of_ a two-word or complex preposition. The course tree
instead gives each word a head: _of_ heads the inner PP and _out_ heads the
larger one. That choice fits its rule that every `PP` has a `P` or `PP` head and
that a PP may be the complement of another PP.

## The grammatical relationships

| Relationship      | What makes it true                                                                                                | Live or approved example                                                         |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Headedness        | The preposition supplies the category of the complete phrase.                                                     | _under that table_ (`c19-a`); _out of the wood_ (`fix-prep-in-prep`)             |
| P complement      | A noun phrase is the common complement of a preposition, but not the only possible one.                           | _under **that table**_ (`c19-a`); _from **the bank**_ (`c19-b`)                  |
| PP complement     | A PP can be the complement of a preposition, creating a nested PP.                                                | _out [of the barn]_ (`c19-c`); _up [through some floorboards]_ (`c19-d`)         |
| PP inside an NP   | A PP can sit inside the noun phrase that contains its head noun; it is not automatically part of the verb phrase. | _The key **to the door**_ (`c19-e`); _The lid **of the box of tools**_ (`c19-f`) |
| External function | A PP's form does not decide whether it is an adverbial, a phrase-internal complement, or a postmodifier.          | Clause-level PPs in `c19-a`–`c19-d`; post-head PPs in `c19-e`–`c19-f`            |
| Attachment        | A following PP can have more than one plausible parent. The words alone need not settle the tree.                 | _along the path beside the river_ (`c19-h`); _her report on the train_ (`c19-i`) |

The last two rows point forward. Lesson 20 tests the difference between a
required and an optional clause-level PP. Lesson 21 names the postmodifier
function, and lesson 27 returns to genuinely ambiguous attachment. Lesson 19
can show that a PP has an inside and an outside without pretending that form
alone settles its job.

## Examples that make the claim visible

`fix-prep-in-prep` is the right central diagram. It displays both levels at
once:

```text
PP                    out of the wood
├── P/head            out
└── PP/complement     of the wood
    ├── P/head        of
    └── NP/complement the wood
```

The learner can see why _out of the wood_ is one PP while _of the wood_ is also
a PP inside it. `c19-c` and `c19-d` repeat that structure in practice without
copying the fixture's sentence.

`c19-e` and `c19-f` are useful for a different reason. They prevent the false
rule that a PP always follows the verb: the PP is inside the subject noun
phrase. But `postmodifier` does not arrive until lesson 21, so these sentences
should demonstrate phrase boundaries now rather than ask the learner to name
that later function. The revision should keep that scope boundary explicit.

`c19-h` and `c19-i` are not clean examples of nesting. _Beside the river_ can
modify _path_ or modify _walked_; _on the train_ can modify _report_ or _read_.
They are valuable only if marked as open readings and deferred to lesson 27.
They cannot prove a single attachment in a lesson whose labels do not yet offer
both analyses.

## Diagnostics and their limits

| Evidence a learner can use                                                                           | What it supports here                                                                                           | Where it stops working                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Replace the complement: _out of the wood_ → _out of it_.                                             | _the wood_ is an NP complement of _of_; the inner PP remains intact.                                            | A pronoun replacement tests a candidate NP boundary. It does not show that every complement is an NP or identify the PP's external function.                                                                                              |
| Front the whole PP: _Out of the wood came the fox._                                                  | The complete sequence can occur as one fronted constituent.                                                     | Failure to front a sequence does not prove that it is not a constituent: information structure, verb choice, and construction can block fronting. A successful fronting test also does not decide attachment in every ambiguous sentence. |
| Make a question or answer with the whole phrase: _Where did the fox come from?_ — _Out of the wood._ | The complete PP can fill a phrase-sized answer position.                                                        | A question can target a smaller constituent too, and the answer identifies a boundary, not a function such as adverbial or postmodifier.                                                                                                  |
| Coordinate complete PPs: _from the bank and from the school_.                                        | Like-category coordination supports treating each complete run as a PP.                                         | Coordination is possible only when meaning and style make the pair natural; a failed coordination test is not decisive.                                                                                                                   |
| Move or replace only the inner NP.                                                                   | In _out of it_, the replacement leaves _of_ with its complement, distinguishing the NP from the surrounding PP. | English permits preposition stranding in some questions and relatives: _Which wood did the fox come out of?_ A separated preposition is not proof that _out_ or _of_ never heads a PP.                                                    |

Movement, question formation, coordination, and replacement are converging
evidence for constituency. None is a definition of PP. The course should avoid
the current implication that a valid PP boundary can never leave any part
behind: English preposition stranding is a counterexample in the right
construction.

## One-word and multi-word complications

The course's normal examples have a preposition with an overt complement, and
that is the right narrow teaching target here. It should nevertheless say
**usually** or **often**, not _every preposition takes a noun phrase_. Modern
descriptions also analyse forms such as _up_ in _She looked up_ and _before_ in
_We had met before_ as intransitive prepositions: they head a PP without an
overt complement. Traditional accounts often call such forms particles or
adverbs instead.

This is a framework decision with a course consequence. The app currently
reserves a verb-attached _up_ for `Part/particle` in lesson 25; lesson 19 has no
approved bare-PP example. Do not introduce the intransitive-preposition
analysis to learners here unless the model and lesson 25 are reconciled first.
Record the limitation instead: a complement is the normal pattern illustrated
in this lesson, not the definition of a preposition.

Likewise, do not call _out of_ and _up through_ two-word prepositions on a page
whose diagram displays nested PPs. That wording gives the learner two
incompatible structures. If a later course decision prefers a single
multi-word-P analysis, change the fixture, learner copy, and practice notes
together.

## Common summaries, and what they omit

| Common summary                                                           | What it gets right                                                | What it leaves out                                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “A prepositional phrase begins with a preposition and ends with a noun.” | It identifies the common _P + NP_ pattern.                        | It makes a frequent complement type into a definition, misses pronouns, clauses, PP complements, and intransitive PPs, and treats the end word as the boundary.             |
| “A preposition shows place or direction.”                                | Spatial relations are a major use.                                | _before the dawn_ (`c19-g`) is temporal, and PPs also express other relations. Meaning does not identify the category.                                                      |
| “A PP is a preposition plus its object.”                                 | It points to a head–complement relation.                          | “Object of a preposition” is another name for the complement in traditional grammar; the summary hides nested PPs and non-NP complements.                                   |
| “A PP is an adverbial phrase.”                                           | Many clause-level PPs are adverbials.                             | A PP can modify a noun or complete an adjective, noun, or verb. Form and function are separate questions.                                                                   |
| “_Out of_ is one two-word preposition.”                                  | The two words often travel together and can express one relation. | It conceals the nested analysis the course actually draws: _out_ takes the PP _of the wood_.                                                                                |
| “The first word tells you where the PP starts.”                          | It is often a good first place to look.                           | A word such as _to_ can be an infinitival marker, and a P-like word can be a verb particle. Category comes from structure and distribution, not spelling or position alone. |

## Corpus audit and shortcut register

The ten live practice sentences cover a better range than the earlier dossier
reported: eight outer PPs attach at clause level, two occur inside subject NPs, and
two sentences contain nested PPs. `c19-g` also blocks the place-only summary.
The set still has a serious instructional tension: its only two ambiguous
attachments are embedded in a lesson that has not yet introduced the functions
needed to contrast the readings.

| Shortcut                                             | Why the current set invites it                        | What defeats it                                                                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “A PP is one preposition plus one noun phrase.”      | Most entries use that common shape.                   | `c19-c`, `c19-d`, and `fix-prep-in-prep` show `PP > P + PP`.                                               |
| “Two adjacent P-looking words make one preposition.” | _out of_ and _up through_ appear in the practice set. | The central diagram must visibly label both P heads and the PP complement.                                 |
| “Every PP is an adverbial after the verb.”           | Eight outer PPs are clause-level.                     | `c19-e` and `c19-f` put PPs inside subject NPs, though their `postmodifier` label must wait for lesson 21. |
| “A PP is about place.”                               | Place meanings dominate the set.                      | `c19-g`, _before the dawn_, supplies a temporal PP.                                                        |
| “The last PP belongs to the verb.”                   | `c19-h` and `c19-i` can be read that way.             | Their competing noun-phrase attachments must be shown only when lesson 27 can compare both trees.          |

## Revision implications

1. **Lead with headedness and nesting, not length.** “Depth” is a property of
   the current examples, not the grammatical generalization. Explain the
   `P/head` and `complement` relation that creates the depth.
2. **Keep `fix-prep-in-prep` as the diagram, but revise its caption and nearby
   prose.** Say that _out_ heads the outer PP and _of the wood_ is its PP
   complement. Remove “two-word preposition,” which contradicts the diagram.
3. **Qualify the complement claim.** “A preposition often takes a noun-phrase
   complement” is accurate for the lesson's starting pattern. Do not teach it
   as an exceptionless definition, and do not add bare-P examples without
   resolving lesson 25's particle analysis.
4. **Use two diagnostics as evidence, not a universal procedure.** The
   complement substitution plus whole-PP fronting contrast is good for the
   fixture. Add the limits on fronting and stranding; do not claim that a true
   PP can never be separated.
5. **Show the two levels of function without naming the later one.** Use
   `c19-e` or a dedicated fixture to show a PP inside a noun phrase, then say
   lesson 21 will name its external job. This prepares lesson 20's
   form/function distinction honestly.
6. **Remove ambiguous attachment from the explanatory proof.** Keep `c19-h`
   and `c19-i` only as marked preview cases, or move them to lesson 27 in a
   future practice revision. Do not present either current parse as uniquely
   established.
7. **Keep the temporal counterexample.** _Before the dawn_ prevents a
   place-only rule with little extra complexity.

## Rejected

- **Defining a PP as a preposition plus a noun phrase.** It is a useful first
  pattern, but the course's own nested fixture and the sources show that it is
  not the category's full definition.
- **Treating _out of_ as a two-word preposition in this course.** That is a
  defensible analysis elsewhere but contradicts the `P > PP` structure shown by
  the current fixture.
- **Calling every PP an adverbial.** The live set includes PPs inside noun
  phrases, and the next lessons are built to distinguish form from function.
- **Using movement as a decisive test.** It provides good positive evidence in
  the fixture, but English permits stranding and restricts fronting for reasons
  unrelated to constituency.
