## Central generalization

An **interjection** is a word-class label; a **supplement** is a structural
function. In _Oh, the gate opened_, _oh_ is an interjection and its job is a
supplement: it is not selected by _opened_ and fills no subject, object,
complement, or adverbial slot. An adverb phrase can have the same
outside-the-clause job: _Unfortunately, the engine stalled._

The lesson must not turn either half into the other. Some interjections occur as
whole utterances, and many sentence-edge expressions are not interjections.
Likewise, a phrase can be a supplement without occurring at the edge: its
detachment, scope, and lack of integration matter more than its first, middle,
or final position.

This is a course-model choice, not a claim that every grammar uses these labels
or draws the boundary identically. The course uses **form: Interj** for the word
class and **function: supplement** for material that sits outside its
clause-frame functions. It follows the broad modern distinction between a
syntactically integrated unit and a detached supplement, while keeping the
learner-facing name short.

## Integration, scope, and evidence

The practical contrast is between words that help build the event or situation
described and words that comment on a larger piece of talk. _Yesterday_ gives a
time for the children's waiting, so it is inside the clause's interpretation.
_Sadly_ evaluates the proposition that they waited. _Frankly_ comments on how
the speaker presents the assertion. That difference in **scope** explains why
the same word class can receive two different functions.

In speech, detached material is often in its own intonation group or set off by
a pause; in writing, commas, dashes, or parentheses may record that separation.
Those are useful clues, but written punctuation does not supply a constituent or
make a word an interjection. Lesson 39 must keep that conclusion: punctuation
can prompt a structural question, but it cannot decide it alone.

Position is evidence of a different kind. Corpus work finds that interjections,
especially _oh_, strongly prefer utterance-initial position, and the live
_surprisingly_ trio correctly counters the old all-initial practice set. A
preference is not a definition, however. _Yesterday_ is also initial or final
in ordinary use, and some discourse markers have strong initial preferences.
Moving an expression can change its emphasis or turn an event-oriented reading
into a speaker-oriented one. Do not say that supplements can go almost anywhere
or that adverbials cannot move.

| Ask whether it supplies a circumstance of the event: _When did the children wait? Yesterday._          | In this controlled pair, a time answer supports an integrated adverbial analysis.                                                        | The old how, when, or where test misses causes, conditions, frequency, degree, and many other adverbial meanings. A failed question does not automatically prove supplement status.       |
| Ask what proposition or act it qualifies: _Sadly, the children waited_; _Frankly, everyone hesitated._ | It draws attention to content-oriented versus speaker-oriented scope and supports the course's detached reading.                         | Scope depends on meaning and context. It is evidence a learner reasons with, not a mechanical labeler.                                                                                    |
| Read the punctuation and, when hearing the sentence, the prosody.                                      | Commas or a separate intonation group support detachment and can tell the reader where to test.                                          | Writers do not punctuate uniformly; an intended supplement can be unmarked, and a comma can mark many other structures. | Compare the same wording in a controlled positional contrast.                                          | The three _surprisingly_ examples show that the intended stance reading is not created by initial position.                              | Mobility is lexical and context-sensitive. No one test defines a supplement. The page should teach a short sequence: first
find the clause frame; then ask whether the candidate fills a role in the event
or instead comments on the claim or its delivery; finally use wording,
punctuation, and position as supporting evidence. This avoids presenting
intuition as a rule while still giving the learner something observable to do.

## Interjections, discourse markers, and vocatives

**Interjections are not defined by their position outside the frame.** The
research literature treats them as a broad and heterogeneous class, often
expressing a reaction, attitude, attention signal, or response. Many are
especially common in speech and may stand alone. _Oh_ is a reasonable clear
course example of a primary reaction interjection; it is not an adverb phrase.
An exhaustive list would be a false shortcut because the class is open-ended and
varies across varieties.

**Well was not a clean second interjection example.** In initial conversational
use it is widely described as a discourse marker, and accounts disagree on its
word class: it can also be an adverb, adjective, response marker, or
turn-management device. The former **c38-b**, _Well, the clerk waited_, forced
that use into the interjection form. The revision replaces it with _Wow, the
clerk returned_, an ordinary reaction interjection. This removes the disputed
word-class choice from graded practice without implying that every _well_ is an
interjection.

**Discourse marker is a function across talk, not a word class in this model.**
_Well_, _anyway_, _so_, _I mean_, and _frankly_ can link, manage, or qualify a
conversation beyond one clause. The present model has neither a discourse
relation nor a discourse-marker label. Do not silently label all of them as
interjections, conjunctions, or supplements; use a simple propositional or
speaker-oriented supplement only when the approved parse actually shows that
relation.

**Vocatives belong beside this topic but outside this lesson's scored claim.**
In _Mara, wait!_, _Mara_ addresses a listener rather than filling the clause's
subject or object slot. The rules can attach a noun-phrase supplement, but the
model has no vocative form or function and no way to state the addressee
relation. Do not add a vocative to the lesson as though the existing diagram
distinguishes it. If the course later teaches it, it needs a named model
decision and an approved prosody/punctuation contrast; familiar spoken
vocatives are not reliably comma-marked.

## Practice and shortcut audit

The live set has two interjection analyses (**c38-a** _Oh_ and **c38-b** _Wow_),
seven adverb-phrase supplements (**c38-c** through **c38-f**, **c38-h** through
**c38-j**), and one integrated fronted adverbial (**c38-g** _Yesterday_). Eight
of ten examples begin with the candidate; one is medial and one final. The
repeated _surprisingly_ trio is a good controlled contrast, but it produces
three of the seven adverb-phrase supplements and every main clause but
**c38-j** is SV. The set therefore still makes a one-word opening plus comma
too easy for a learner who has not met the middle and final examples.

The current fixtures cover both menu decisions: **fix-interjection** visibly
covers the interjection form, and **fix-supplement** visibly covers the
supplement function. The grammar permits a supplement on a sentence or clause
as an adverb phrase, adjective phrase, preposition phrase, noun phrase, clause,
or interjection, and also permits a supplementary clause on a noun phrase. The
lesson currently demonstrates only an interjection and an adverb phrase as
sentence supplements. That is an honest narrow scope; do not imply that every
possible supplement form is a lesson-38 target. In particular, the supplementary
relative now used in lesson 39 and the appositive from lesson 22 must remain
their own constructions.

## Revision direction

1. Open with the form/function split: _Oh_ is an interjection; _oh_ and
   _unfortunately_ can both be supplements. Do not define an interjection as
   outside the grammar.
2. Keep the fixture contrast and the _Yesterday_ contrast, but revise the
   procedure so removal is one clue, not the decision. Explain scope in plain
   language: a time word helps describe the event; a stance word comments on
   the whole claim or on saying it.
3. Keep the three-position _surprisingly_ small multiple, but caption it as a
   controlled example of one reading, not proof of free movement. State that
   initial position and commas are common evidence, not definitions.
4. Keep revised **c38-b**, _Wow, the clerk returned_, as the second clear
   primary-interjection example. Do not restore _well_ as evidence for a word
   class that authoritative accounts dispute.
5. Do not add vocatives or discourse-connective adverbs to graded practice
   until the model can represent their distinct relations. A brief limit note is
   more accurate than a forced label.
6. Keep the forward connection to lesson 39: commas and pauses can support a
   supplement reading, while structure and intended scope decide it. Link
   backward to lesson 18's integrated adverbials, not to lesson 37's passive
   voice.

prosody and punctuation often show detachment. They do not license a simple
word-list or a categorical treatment of every utterance-initial item, especially
_well_. The course should state its interjection/supplement analysis as a useful
model, give clear _Oh_ and stance-adverb examples, and mark its boundaries.

## Rejected as lesson content

- **Interjections stand outside the grammar.** This turns a possible supplement
  function into a definition of a word class and excludes standalone responses
  and other uses.
- **A supplement can go almost anywhere; an adverbial cannot.** Both claims
  overstate a positional tendency and hide scope changes.
- **A comma proves a supplement.** It is written evidence for a possible
  prosodic break, not a grammatical function.
- **Remove it and see.** Omission establishes optionality, which many integrated
  adverbials share.
- **Every _well_ here is an interjection.** This ignores the discourse-marker
  analysis and the category disagreement documented by the sources.
