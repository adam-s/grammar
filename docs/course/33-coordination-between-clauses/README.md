## The central generalization

**Clause coordination relates two or more clauses of equal syntactic status;
neither clause fills a role inside the other.** The clauses are the
**coordinates**. A coordinator such as _and_, _or_, or _but_ marks the relation;
it is not a third coordinate. The coordination therefore has no ordinary head,
predicate, or clause kind of its own.

This continues lesson 26's analysis at clause level. In _The engine stalled and
the car stopped_, neither clause is the other's object, modifier, or complement.
Compare _The engine stalled because the car stopped_: the _because_-clause is
subordinate, relating to the main clause rather than standing as its equal
partner.

The course displays the coordinator as a child between two coordinate clauses.
Other grammars may place it inside an expanded coordinate instead. That tree
choice must not be taught as a universal fact. Both analyses agree that the
coordinator is not a third coordinate and that ordinary coordination is not
headed by either coordinate.

## The grammar behind the claim

### Equal relation, not an independent-clause definition

The familiar rule “two independent clauses joined by a coordinating
conjunction” describes the ten live practice sentences: each has two finite
clauses with overt subjects, and either full clause can be written as a
sentence. It is evidence for their analysis, not the definition of
coordination.

English also coordinates subordinate and non-finite clauses when the relevant
status is shared: _You can phone if I am late or if I am not coming_, and _to
receive calls but not to make them_. It can omit recoverable material: _Kim
sang and danced_ shares the subject, and _Mara packed the tent and Sam the
stove_ has **gapping**, an omitted verb in the second coordinate. A learner
cannot turn the standalone test into a rule that every coordinated clause must
pass.

The course should make a narrower, earned claim: in its full-clause examples,
separating the coordinates gives two complete written sentences. That supports
equal status and contrasts helpfully with lesson 29's marked adverbial clause.
It does not define coordination or dependency. As lesson 28 establishes,
subordinate status is a structural relationship, not merely an inability to
stand alone.

### Relation, meaning, order, and scope

_And_, _or_, and _but_ mark the same structural relation but contribute
different meanings: addition or sequence, alternatives, and contrast. _The snow
melted and the river rose_ naturally suggests sequence or cause. Reversing the
clauses can change that reading while leaving them coordinated. Swapping is
therefore a weak observation about rank, never a definition or diagnostic.

The coordinator normally occurs between its coordinates. That linear fact
contrasts with a fronted adverbial clause in lesson 29, but does not establish
the whole analysis. Coordinators can be absent in special asyndetic
constructions, so a word scan is only a shortcut for this course's marked
examples. Clause order, information structure, and coordinator meaning can
affect scope without making one coordinate the head of the other.

### Boundaries, headedness, and punctuation

The boundary is around the whole coordination. In the coordination fixture, the
outer sentence contains a first coordinate clause, the coordinator, and a second
coordinate clause. The outer unit does not predicate anything itself; each inner
clause has its own subject and predicate. The course audits therefore exempt the
coordination from a verb-type and head question while auditing each coordinate
separately.

This is the same headless relation lesson 26 introduces. Lesson 33 should reuse
**coordinate** and **coordinator**, not rename the pattern as an _and_ sentence
or a “compound sentence.” Lesson 27's scope work remains relevant: structural
rank alone does not settle meanings contributed by sequence, contrast,
condition, or cause.

The comma in _The mechanic repaired the engine, and the car started_ is not a
coordinate, coordinator, or node in the tree. It can help a reader see a
boundary in formal writing; its absence from a short example does not remove
coordination, and a comma by itself does not create it. The current lesson's
comma and non-comma fixtures deliberately have the same
coordinate/coordinator structure. Keep that contrast for lesson 39, but do not
teach a comma as a structural requirement.

## Evidence a learner can use

No single diagnostic defines coordination. A revision can give this procedure
for the ordinary full-clause pattern and state each limit beside it.

1. Mark the whole first and second subject--predicate frames. In _The kettle
   boiled and the lights dimmed_, each span contains its own finite predicate.
2. Check whether neither span fills a job inside the other. Each full span can
   make a sentence here, which supports equal status. By contrast, _because the
   gate was locked_ relates to a main clause rather than becoming its equal.
3. Mark _and_, _or_, or _but_ as the coordinator between the spans, not as one
   of the spans. Then check what meaning it contributes: adding, choosing, or
   contrasting.

| Evidence                                            | What it supports here                                               | Where it stops                                                                                                    |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Two complete finite frames                          | The live examples contain two full clause coordinates.              | One clause may contain several verb words, and coordinates can share or omit a subject, verb, or later material.  |
| Each full coordinate can be a sentence              | Equal status in the practice set; a useful contrast with lesson 29. | It is not a definition of coordination or a reliable definition of main versus dependent status.                  |
| A coordinator between two spans                     | A visible cue to this course's marked examples.                     | _And_ can join phrases; coordinators may be absent, and a different linker may introduce a subordinate clause.    |
| Punctuation changes while the fixture tree does not | A comma can support a boundary without changing the relation.       | Commas are editorial evidence, not a grammatical node or decisive test.                                           |
| Parallel-looking clause frames                      | Parallelism reinforces an ordinary coordinate reading.              | Gapping, ellipsis, right-node raising, and unlike-category coordination make it an unreliable constituency proof. |

Do not teach fronting, coordinator movement, or reversing the coordinates as
diagnostics. The movement behavior of a marked adverbial clause does not give an
equivalent test for coordination, and event order or focus can make reversal
awkward or change the meaning.

## Course-model limits that the page must respect

The model gives a coordinator the form Conj and function coordinator; its
clausal partners have function coordinate. A coordinate clause has no
clause-kind label, because it is not a nominal, relative, adverbial, or
comparative clause. The outer coordination has no ordinary head or predicate.

The model does not express the source grammar's bare-coordinate versus
expanded-coordinate distinction, and the practice helper accepts only two full
clauses. It can represent VP ellipsis and gapping in separate fixtures, but the
current lesson neither practises them nor has a page figure that explains their
indexed omissions. It also cannot give a truthful ordinary outer category to
unlike-category coordination, an existing lesson-26 limit. Do not introduce
these constructions through a misleading diagram.

Punctuation deliberately stays outside the tree. A comma is a visible part of a
written sentence, not a word class or syntactic function. The model's ability to
accept one between coordinates supports the lesson-39 contrast; it is not a
reason to give the comma a label.

## Common teaching summaries, and what they leave out

| Summary                                            | What it gets right                                                   | What must be added or qualified                                                                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “A coordinating conjunction joins equal things.”   | Coordination relates units of equal status.                          | _Conjunction_ is a word class; **coordinator** is its job, and it is not a coordinate. Equal status is structural, not equal importance. |
| “A compound sentence has two independent clauses.” | It describes these ten full-clause examples.                         | It misses shared subjects, ellipsis, gapping, non-finite coordination, and coordinated subordinate clauses.                              |
| “_And_, _but_, and _or_ join clauses.”             | These are the visible coordinators the course uses.                  | The same words can join phrases and coordinators can relate material other than clauses.                                                 |
| “A comma goes before a coordinating conjunction.”  | It often describes formal writing with two full independent clauses. | It is a convention with conditions, not syntax; short coordinates and other constructions differ.                                        |
| “Coordinates can switch places.”                   | Neither coordinate is normally the other's head.                     | Reversal can change time, cause, focus, idiomaticity, or the intended reading.                                                           |
| “Coordination proves constituency.”                | Parallel ordinary coordinates give converging evidence.              | Gapping and right-node raising make it unreliable as a standalone test.                                                                  |

## Shortcuts and revision direction

| Shortcut                                         | Current evidence                                                | What should defeat or qualify it                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Every _and_ is clause coordination.              | 6/10 practice sentences use _and_.                              | Reuse lesson 26's phrase coordination in prose, then identify the two predicate frames in the clause fixture. |
| Every _or_ or _but_ is a different structure.    | The set includes both, but only one _or_.                       | State that their meanings differ while the coordinate/coordinator relation stays the same.                    |
| A coordinator always joins two full sentences.   | All 10 practice sentences have overt subjects and finite verbs. | Put shared-subject coordination and gapping beside the standalone procedure as limits.                        |
| A comma decides whether clauses are coordinated. | Practice has none; the paired fixture has one.                  | Keep the fixture contrast and say that neither tree changes.                                                  |
| Two clauses must be coordination.                | Earlier lessons teach nominal and adverbial clauses.            | Contrast _because the gate was locked_: equal partner versus a clause related to a main clause.               |
| Coordinates can always be swapped.               | Several _and_ examples invite a temporal reading.               | Remove swapping as a learner diagnostic.                                                                      |

The eventual revision should:

1. Lead with the equal-status relationship and the coordination fixture, reusing
   the terminology from lesson 26.
2. Use the full-sentence split as evidence for that fixture, then immediately
   limit it to the displayed full-clause pattern. Replace the current absolute
   claim that each side can stand alone.
3. Contrast coordination with a marked lesson-29 adverbial clause or the
   lesson-28 nominal-clause analysis, without reducing subordination to a word
   list.
4. Keep the punctuation fixture as the controlled visual pair, but leave writing
   conventions to lesson 39 and avoid saying a comma is required.
5. Do not reveal the complete parses of graded practice sentences. The fixtures
   already supply the diagram evidence.
6. Keep the practice set unless a later revision needs another _or_ or a
   shared-subject limit. Any addition needs an accepted parse and must not make
   ellipsis an unprepared label decision.

## Claims to keep, qualify, replace, or remove

| Current learner-facing claim                                                                   | Decision              | Reason                                                                                                                |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “Clause coordination joins two clauses of equal status.”                                       | **Keep.**             | It states the central relation and agrees with lesson 26.                                                             |
| “A coordinator joins them without making either dependent on the other.”                       | **Keep, qualify.**    | It contrasts coordination and subordination, but should name the relation rather than make independence a definition. |
| “Each side can stand alone.”                                                                   | **Qualify.**          | It is sound evidence for the fixture and practice set, not every coordinated clause.                                  |
| “Make two sentences.”                                                                          | **Keep, narrow.**     | It is useful for this full-clause fixture if its evidential status and limit stay visible.                            |
| “A comma helps mark the boundary, but both clauses and the coordinator remain the same forms.” | **Keep.**             | The paired fixtures visibly establish this point.                                                                     |
| “Coordinated clauses cannot swap freely.”                                                      | **Remove as a test.** | Meaningful order is real, but it does not identify coordination.                                                      |
| “The coordinator cannot move.”                                                                 | **Remove as a test.** | It confuses between-coordinate position with the movement behavior of a subordinate clause.                           |

## Rejected for this lesson

- **A universal independent-clause rule.** It turns a good observation about
  the live full clauses into a false definition and hides sharing and ellipsis.
- **A comma rule.** Keep the structure contrast here; teach formal punctuation
  conventions in lesson 39.
- **Swapping or coordinator movement as a procedure.** Neither reliably
  diagnoses coordination.
- **Gapping, VP ellipsis, right-node raising, or unlike-category coordination
  as new practice.** They are necessary limits on the first explanation, but
  exceed the live lesson's practice and diagram coverage.
