# Lesson 33 — Coordination between clauses

Researched 28 August 2026. An author's dossier. See
[../01-introduction/README.md](../01-introduction/README.md).

## What the lesson decides

**Nothing new.** `Conj`, `coordinate` and `coordinator` all arrived at lesson 26. This lesson raises them from phrases to clauses.

## The finding

Three distinct asked-shapes, seven of ten verb-final, and the lesson is sound as
far as it goes. Seven _and_, three _but_, no _or_.

**No sentence has a comma**, which matters because lesson 39 is built almost
entirely on comma-plus-coordinator sentences of exactly this shape. The two
lessons are a controlled pair, and lesson 39 has the comma while lesson 33 does
not.

That pairing is deliberate and good. It is also the only punctuation contrast in
the course, and it is spread across six lessons.

## The tests

**Both halves stand alone.** _The kettle boiled._ and _The lights dimmed._ are
each sentences. That is what separates coordination from subordination, where
one half cannot.

**No reordering.** Coordinated clauses cannot swap freely when the order carries
meaning, unlike coordinated noun phrases. _The pipe froze and the ceiling
cracked_ is not the same as the reverse.

**The coordinator cannot move.** Unlike an adverbial clause's marker, which
travels with its clause to the front, _and_ is stuck between the two.

## Shortcut register

| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| _and_ joins two clauses               | _but_, _or_                            | partly — no _or_                          |

## What this should change

1. **Add _or_.** Two of the three coordinators appear.
2. **Nothing else.** With lesson 39 as its pair, this lesson is doing its job.
