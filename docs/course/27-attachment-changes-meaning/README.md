## What the lesson decides

Nothing new enters the label menu. The lesson asks the learner to use labels
already in scope to make a harder decision: which larger constituent contains
the final phrase or modifier.

For the PP cases, the relevant distinction is already available:

| Structure   | When the final PP is under it                                        | Course label for the PP |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| verb phrase | It describes the event, such as the instrument or place of watching. | `adverbial`             |
| noun phrase | It helps identify the noun phrase's referent.                        | `postmodifier`          |

For the final two cases, the question is how far an adjective reaches in a
coordination. _Old_ can modify _men_ alone, or the coordinated _men and women_.
That is attachment and scope, not PP attachment.

## Central generalization

**One string of words can allow more than one ordinary tree, and the trees can
make different claims.** In _She watched the boy with the binoculars_, _with
the binoculars_ can sit in the verb phrase and say how she watched, or inside
the object noun phrase and say which boy she watched. Neither word order nor
the preposition itself chooses between those structures.

The choice is often resolved in use by lexical meaning, the surrounding
discourse, a shared situation, or speech rhythm. Those are clues to what a
speaker meant, not a rule that changes one tree into the other. If the supplied
words and context leave both readings ordinary, the honest analysis preserves
both; it does not invent one intended tree.

This lesson is the course's first sustained practice with stored structural
alternatives, not the course's only ambiguity. Lesson 37 models the
state/passive contrast in _The gates were closed_, and lesson 40 returns to PP
attachment. Do not claim that lesson 27 is uniquely ambiguous or that all
ambiguity is noun-versus-verb PP attachment.

## The grammatical relationships

`fix-ambiguous` makes the central PP contrast visible without changing any
words:

```text
I saw [the man] [with the telescope].
             ^ PP attaches to the verb phrase: I used a telescope.

I saw [the man [with the telescope]].
                 ^ PP attaches inside the object NP: the man had one.
```

The first tree has the PP as an adverbial sibling of the direct object under the
verb phrase. The second has the PP as a postmodifier under the nominal headed
by _man_. A diagram must show the different parents. Highlighting the same last
three words twice does not show an attachment difference.

The coordination cases need a separate contrast:

```text
the [[old men] and women]       only the men are old
the [old [men and women]]       both the men and the women are old
```

Here the adjective has different scope because it occupies a different level in
the nominal. It is not enough to say that _old_ "applies to" one or two nouns:
the contrast must show whether it modifies a coordinate or the coordination as
a whole.

These are structural ambiguities, not cases where one word has two unrelated
dictionary entries. Emerson and Copestake's PP examples make the same point: a
final PP can modify the preceding noun or the main verb even when the available
syntactic options are the same. What differs is the semantic relation the tree
expresses.

## What can help, and where it stops

No constituency diagnostic chooses an intended reading when both candidate
trees are grammatical and make sense. Diagnostics can test the consequences of
a proposed tree; they cannot read a speaker's mind.

| Evidence to try                                                                                                  | What it can show                                                                                          | Its limit                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Substitute the whole object: _She watched him._ / _She watched him with the binoculars._                         | Once a reading is proposed, the material that disappears or remains identifies the candidate NP boundary. | Both remainders are grammatical. The test displays each analysis; it does not select one. Pronouns also change case, number, reference, and sometimes meaning.                         |
| Give the two paraphrases: _She used the binoculars to watch him_ / _She watched the boy who had the binoculars._ | Each paraphrase makes the distinct relation easy to state.                                                | A paraphrase is evidence about a chosen interpretation, not an independent structural test. It can add or lose information.                                                            |
| Ask _How did she watch?_ or _Which boy?_                                                                         | In a clear setting, the questions draw attention to the two meanings.                                     | The setting supplies the answer. Question form does not prove that the bare string had only that reading.                                                                              |
| Front a proposed event PP: _With the binoculars, she watched the boy._                                           | A successful fronting can support treating the PP as a clause-level constituent in that reading.          | Fronting is sensitive to information structure, style, and particular verbs. A failed movement is not proof of noun attachment, and a successful one does not erase the other reading. |
| Coordinate or replace a complete phrase.                                                                         | A successful coordination or replacement can support a candidate constituent boundary.                    | Coordination needs a natural semantic match, and replacement can be licensed for reasons other than the proposed tree. Neither test settles attachment by itself.                      |
| Remove the final phrase.                                                                                         | The remainder can make a smaller phrase visible.                                                          | A grammatical remainder does not distinguish a modifier from a complement, prove that the omitted phrase had one parent, or preserve the original meaning.                             |

The lesson page should call these converging clues. In its core example, their
proper result is: "Here are two trees and two meanings; now ask the context
which one the speaker intended." It should not promise a mechanical procedure
that returns one answer for every PP after an object.

## Meaning, context, punctuation, and speech

The words commonly make one reading more plausible. _The sheriff shot a dog
with a rifle_ strongly invites the rifle as an instrument; _The dog bit a
sheriff with a moustache_ strongly invites it as a property of the sheriff.
That preference comes from the meanings and typical relations of _shot_,
_rifle_, _sheriff_, and _moustache_, not from a different syntactic pattern.
Discourse and a visible situation can override a general lexical preference:
if the speaker is choosing among several boys, _with the binoculars_ may
identify one of them.

Punctuation and prosody can guide an audience, but neither is a universal tree
test. The unpunctuated written examples remain structurally open. A comma or
parenthetical punctuation may create a different construction or signal a
speaker's intended grouping; it is not a neutral annotation that reveals a
pre-existing answer. In speech, a pause or intonational boundary can influence
the chosen interpretation, but the syntax-prosody relationship is systematic
and imperfect. Speakers may provide no useful cue when they are unaware of the
ambiguity or when context already makes their meaning clear. Treat punctuation
and prosody as additional evidence when actually supplied, never as grounds to
say that the words alone have one tree.

## Common summaries and what they leave out

| Summary                                               | Useful part                                                               | Missing limit                                                                                                            |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| “The PP attaches to the nearest noun.”                | It notices one possible local grouping.                                   | False in verb attachment, and distance is not a definition of a parent-child relation.                                   |
| “A PP after an object tells how the action happened.” | It describes one common event reading.                                    | The same PP can instead identify the object noun.                                                                        |
| “Context decides the grammar.”                        | Context often tells listeners which meaning was intended.                 | Context selects among available analyses; it does not make the other grammatical structure disappear.                    |
| “Use pronoun substitution to find the answer.”        | The two replacement results can expose candidate boundaries.              | It must be applied to a proposed reading, and both analyses may pass.                                                    |
| “There is no test.”                                   | No structural test alone settles intended meaning in a genuine ambiguity. | It should not imply that analysis is guesswork: the competing trees, their spans, and their consequences are observable. |
| “All attachment ambiguity is PP attachment.”          | PP attachment is a clear first case.                                      | Coordination scope in `c27-i` and `c27-j` is a second kind in this very lesson.                                          |

## Current corpus, fixtures, and shortcuts

The ten current practice sentences divide into two real ambiguity types:

| Items           | Structure represented                                                  | Reading contrast                                                  |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `c27-a`–`c27-h` | final PP under the VP or inside the direct-object NP                   | event/instrument/location reading versus noun-identifying reading |
| `c27-i`–`c27-j` | adjective inside the first coordinate or above the coordinated nominal | adjective reaches the first noun only versus both nouns           |

Each entry holds two readings, with distinct glosses. The PP helper builds
`PP/adverbial` under the verb phrase and `PP/postmodifier` inside the object
nominal. The scope helper builds the two distinct adjective positions. Course
scope tests replay every reading through the lesson-27 palette; sentence audits
check every reading for well-formedness and for distinct glosses. These checks
prove that the model can represent, render, and grade either structural choice.
They do **not** prove that a constructed sentence has been judged natural or
that one reading was intended. The repository records all course sentences as
unreviewed.

The learner page cites `fix-ambiguous`, and that fixture stores both PP
readings. Its grading and layout tests specifically check that the alternate
attachment is accepted and has a different depth. `fix-coordinated-nominal`
now stores both coordination readings — wide scope as canonical and narrow
scope as an audited alternate — and the page draws them side by side by naming
each stored reading (`readingId`). Both scope trees on the page therefore come
from accepted model data rather than from a hand-drawn comparison. Like every
stored reading, the narrow alternate awaits human review.

The current practice set has useful variety, but it also invites shortcuts:

| Shortcut                                             | Why it is tempting                                                                          | What the lesson or a future set must make clear                                                                                                           |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “Every ambiguity is a final PP.”                     | Eight items share `subject + transitive verb + object + PP`; the two scope cases come last. | Put the coordination-scope contrast on the page, and name it as another attachment decision.                                                              |
| “The noun just before the PP wins.”                  | Every PP item presents a nearby object noun and a final PP.                                 | Hold the words still in `fix-ambiguous` and show the PP under two different parents.                                                                      |
| “Choose the canonical tree.”                         | The data implementation marks one reading `canonical` and the other `alternate`.            | Explain that these are storage and feedback statuses, not claims about intended meaning or linguistic superiority.                                        |
| “The paraphrase tells me which answer is right.”     | Each item supplies a gloss for each reading.                                                | Use the paraphrases to compare meanings, not as a hidden answer key. Both must remain accepted when the prompt supplies no disambiguating context.        |
| “A corpus's most frequent attachment is the answer.” | Corpus-based parsers often learn useful lexical and co-occurrence preferences.              | Frequency is a preference under a corpus and genre, not a rule for a new utterance; rare word combinations and new contexts produce sparse-data failures. |

The lesson may use a corpus or model to _find_ examples of ambiguity, but it
must not use a parser's highest-ranked analysis as proof of a single intended
tree. PP-attachment research uses lexical heads, co-occurrence, semantic
relations, and world knowledge precisely because structural form alone does not
settle every case. Such systems rank plausible readings; a lesson about genuine
ambiguity should retain the alternatives that its own wording leaves open.

## Revision implications

1. Replace the page's PP-only generalization with the central generalization
   above: one word string can support more than one tree, and the trees make
   different claims. State PP attachment as the main example, not the whole
   definition of ambiguity.
2. Keep `fix-ambiguous` as the main visual, but show both parents directly or
   use two small diagrams. Its current caption says that another reading exists
   while displaying only one; the contrast must be visible.
3. The scope contrast is on the page as a second visual, drawn from the two
   stored readings of `fix-coordinated-nominal` rather than from a graded
   item. This prevents a false claim that ambiguity here always means PP
   attachment.
4. Replace “No structural test chooses the intended reading” with a qualified
   procedure: propose each boundary, use substitution or paraphrase to state
   its consequence, then consult supplied context, punctuation, or speech if
   any exists. If none does, preserve both.
5. Do not expose a single graded practice answer as the page's proof. Prefer
   the fixture for the PP contrast. If the page uses a live scope item, mark it
   as a demonstrated alternative rather than presenting one reading as the
   answer.
6. Do not add an unqualified “nearest noun,” deletion, punctuation, or corpus
   frequency rule. Each is at most a context-sensitive clue.
7. Reconcile the page with lessons 19 and 21: those lessons correctly defer
   _beside the river_, _on the train_, and _with the telescope_ when their
   words leave attachment open. Lesson 27 should make that limit explicit,
   then return the learner to the fact that PP form alone does not determine
   function.
8. Reconcile the forward connection with lesson 28: the next lesson introduces
   a clause inside a larger structure, not another ambiguity test. Do not make
   embedded clauses necessary to understand this page.

## Sources opened and read

- UK Department for Education, [_English glossary_](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7c8e4ded915d48c24108e2/English_Glossary.pdf), pp. 11–12. The official glossary defines a phrase by grammatical connection and a head, says that phrases can contain other phrases, and identifies a prepositional phrase as headed by a preposition with a following complement. It supports the course's structural vocabulary, not a rule for resolving attachment.
- Cambridge University Press & Assessment, [“Noun phrases: dependent words,” _English Grammar Today_](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/noun-phrases-dependent-words). The opened reference distinguishes post-head complements from postmodifiers and gives PP postmodifier examples. It supports the course's noun-internal vocabulary while also showing that the complement/postmodifier boundary is a separate question from mere word order.
- Cambridge University Press & Assessment, [“Prepositional phrases,” _English Grammar Today_](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/prepositional-phrases). The opened reference describes PPs as a preposition plus a complement and shows that PPs can occur after verbs. It supports the form/function separation used in the PP contrast.
- Guy Emerson and Ann Copestake, [“Leveraging a Semantically Annotated Corpus to Disambiguate Prepositional Phrase Attachment”](https://aclanthology.org/W15-0101.pdf), pp. 1–2. The authors contrast a final PP modifying a preceding noun with one modifying a main verb and show that, where the available structures are the same, semantic information is needed to rank parses.
- Patrick Pantel and Dekang Lin, [“An Unsupervised Approach to Prepositional Phrase Attachment using Contextually Similar Words”](https://aclanthology.org/P00-1014.pdf), pp. 1–2. Their _salad with a fork/croutons_ contrast makes the VP-versus-NP distinction concrete and documents the use of lexical and corpus evidence as a ranking method rather than a grammar definition.
- Jesse Snedeker and Elizabeth Casserly, [“Is it all relative? Effects of prosodic boundaries on the comprehension and production of attachment ambiguities”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228866115_Is_it_all_relative_Effects_of_prosodic_boundaries_on_the_comprehension_and_production_of_attachment_ambiguities), pp. 1–5. The opened full text studies PP attachment, finds that prosodic boundary strength influences interpretation, and reports an imperfect syntax-prosody relation.
- Olga van Herwijnen, Jacques Terken, Antal van den Bosch, and Erwin Marsi, [“Learning PP attachment for filtering prosodic phrasing”](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/anthology-files/pdf/E/E03/E03-1051.pdf), pp. 1–2. This study explicitly says there is no one-to-one syntax-prosody mapping and that punctuation-only phrase-boundary assignment yields omissions and errors. Its data are Dutch, so it supports the stated general caution about punctuation and prosody, not an English teaching rule.

## Rejected or qualified claims

- **“This is the only ambiguous lesson in the course.”** False in the live
  source: lesson 37 and lesson 40 also store alternate readings.
- **“Every sentence here is PP attachment.”** False. `c27-i` and `c27-j` are
  coordination-scope ambiguities.
- **“There is no test.”** Too blunt. There is no test that chooses intended
  meaning in every genuine ambiguity, but constituency diagnostics can still
  display and support each proposed analysis.
- **“Context decides the one true tree.”** Too strong. Context can make one
  available tree the intended interpretation; without enough context, both
  structural analyses remain warranted.
- **“Punctuation or a pause settles it.”** Too strong. Both can guide a
  listener or reader when supplied, but their relationship to syntax is not
  one-to-one and the bare written string may remain open.
- **“The model's canonical reading is the right answer.”** False. It is a
  storage default. The grader is designed to accept the alternate reading.
- **“Corpus frequency settles attachment.”** False. Corpus evidence supplies
  useful lexical preferences, but it is sparse, domain-bound, and cannot turn a
  plausible alternative into an ungrammatical tree.
