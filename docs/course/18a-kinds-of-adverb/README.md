## What the lesson decides

**Nothing.** `teaches` is empty and must stay empty.

The page explains a deliberate boundary in the model. `Adv` names a word class,
`AdvP` names a phrase form, and `adverbial` names a function. The model does not
add labels for _manner_, _time_, _place_, or _frequency_. Those are useful ways
to describe what an expression contributes to an interpretation, but they are
not four entries a learner can recover by one structural test.

This does **not** mean that meanings have no grammatical consequences. Position,
attachment, and scope can change what an adverb modifies, and some semantic
groups have strong position preferences. It means only that the app does not
encode a separate syntactic label for each familiar meaning label.

## Central generalization

Labels such as _manner_, _time_, _place_, and _frequency_ sort an adverb or
adverbial by the kind of meaning a reader finds in context. They do not by
themselves say whether the expression is an adverb, an adverb phrase, or an
adverbial, nor do they uniquely determine its attachment or scope.

In a controlled set such as _The crew cleared the track quickly_, _The train
arrived late_, and _The visitors waited outside_, a one-word `AdvP` can have the
same `adverbial` function while adding different kinds of event information. The
tree earns the shared structural label; the interpretation earns _manner_,
_time_, or _place_. The first claim is graded syntax. The second is a discussion
of meaning.

That scope matters. _Yesterday the children played_ is a time expression, but
the course attaches its fronted phrase at the clause level because it scopes over
the whole clause. _The train arrived late_ places a time expression inside the
verb phrase. A page must therefore not say that every time, place, manner, or
frequency expression has “the same tree.” It can say exactly what its chosen,
controlled figures share.

## The relationships the page must keep separate

| Question                           | What the course can show                                                                                                                      | What the semantic label adds                                                                                      |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What kind of word is it?           | _quickly_, _late_, and bare _outside_ can be `Adv`; _outside the station_ is a `P` in this model.                                             | Nothing reliable. The same meaning can be expressed with more than one form.                                      |
| What is the phrase?                | A bare adverb can project an `AdvP`; _very quietly_ is an `AdvP` headed by _quietly_.                                                         | A label such as _manner_ does not draw that boundary.                                                             |
| What job does the phrase do here?  | An `AdvP` can be an `adverbial`; an `Adv` can instead be a `premodifier` inside an `AdjP`.                                                    | It says what sort of circumstance, degree, or comment the reader understands.                                     |
| What does it modify or scope over? | Attachment distinguishes _very quietly_ from _surprisingly narrow_; clause-fronted material has wider course scope than a VP-internal phrase. | The position can change the interpretation, so the same word may need a different paraphrase in another position. |

The course's internal note uses seven illustrative labels: manner, time, place,
frequency, degree, conjunctive, and evaluative. That is a model-design note, not
a settled universal taxonomy. Cambridge's learner grammar groups time, manner,
place, degree, and frequency, then distinguishes further groups by position and
scope. The UNLV grammar chapter also lists duration and number. A learner page
should say “common meaning labels,” not claim that every book agrees on a fixed
number or a clean set of borders.

## Diagnostics, and where they stop

No procedure discovers a semantic kind from syntax alone. The page should use
the following as ways to explain an interpretation, not as a scored procedure.

| Evidence or question                                                                                                         | What it can support                                                                                          | Where it stops                                                                                                                                                                |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ask a meaning question: _How did they clear it?_ — _quickly_; _Where did they wait?_ — _outside_.                            | It makes the intended reading plain.                                                                         | The question assumes the reading it appears to find. A word can answer more than one plausible question, and the answers do not establish a syntactic category or attachment. |
| Replace one expression with a meaning-specific paraphrase: _quickly_ → _in a quick way_; _late_ → _after the expected time_. | It exposes the different contribution to the event.                                                          | The paraphrase may change register, phrase form, emphasis, or scope. It is evidence about interpretation, not a constituent test.                                             |
| Remove the whole final `AdvP`: _The train arrived late_ → _The train arrived_.                                               | In this frame, the phrase is an optional adverbial.                                                          | Removal does not distinguish time from manner or place, and an adverbial can be required by another verb frame. It cannot define either `Adv` or `AdvP`.                      |
| Compare position: _Yesterday the children played_ / _The children played yesterday_.                                         | A time expression may take different positions, and the model can draw a different attachment.               | Position varies with the word, information structure, and intended scope. A preference is not a universal category test.                                                      |
| Compare attachment: _very quietly_ / _surprisingly narrow_.                                                                  | The first complete phrase modifies _waited_; the second adverb modifies _narrow_ inside an adjective phrase. | The test distinguishes targets in these examples. It does not assign every adverb to one semantic class.                                                                      |

Scope is the most important limit to put beside the demonstration. A modifier's
meaning applies to the constituent it attaches to. _Only Dana checked the
ledger_, _Dana only checked the ledger_, and _Dana checked only the ledger_ place
the same word near different material and invite different focus readings. Even
when a sentence diagram is useful, its branches do not by themselves settle all
of those discourse choices. This optional page should mention scope as a reason
to be cautious, not try to teach focus analysis before the course supports it.

## Common summaries, and what they omit

| Common summary                                                      | What it gets right                                                                | What it leaves out                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “An adverb tells how, when, or where.”                              | Manner, time, and place are familiar meanings.                                    | It omits degree, frequency, duration, stance, focus, and discourse-connecting uses. It also turns a meaning clue into a word-class definition. |
| “There are four/six/seven kinds of adverb.”                         | A list can help a reader notice recurring meanings.                               | Lists differ because their criteria and level of detail differ. A number does not establish a syntactic inventory.                             |
| “Adverbs of manner, time, place, and frequency have the same tree.” | Carefully chosen final `AdvP/adverbial` examples can share that course structure. | Position and attachment can differ, and scope can alter the interpretation. The claim is only as broad as the approved examples.               |
| “An adverbial is an adverb.”                                        | Many adverb phrases work as adverbials.                                           | `Adv` is a word class and `adverbial` is a function; a PP or clause can also be adverbial. Lesson 20 must keep that distinction explicit.      |
| “_Twice_ is an adverb of frequency.”                                | It concerns repetition.                                                           | In _checked twice_, it gives an event count. Treating count and habitual frequency as interchangeable creates a false-clean fourth category.   |
| “Words ending in _-ly_ are adverbs of manner.”                      | Many manner adverbs have that spelling.                                           | The clue misses _late_, _outside_, and _twice_, catches adjectives such as _friendly_, and says nothing about function or scope.               |

## Revision and build implications

1. **Keep the model boundary; do not add semantic menu labels.** A category
   inferred only from the reader's interpretation cannot be safely graded in
   this builder. The optional page explains that restraint instead of disguising
   it as a gap.
2. **Revise the proposal before any page is built.** Reclassify _twice_ as
   count/iteration, or propose a natural frequency example such as _The auditor
   checked the ledger often_. The latter still needs a human-accepted reading,
   a fixture, and a decision about whether its position supports the intended
   comparison.
3. **Do not promise four identical trees.** A main small-multiple could use
   approved, clause-final examples that genuinely share `VP > AdvP/adverbial`.
   A second contrast should show why fronted _Yesterday_ and _surprisingly
   narrow_ fall outside that controlled comparison. The captions must name the
   exact attachment shown.
4. **Do not expose existing practice answers.** `c18-b`, `c18-h`, and the
   earlier _outside_ examples establish what is built, but a static lesson needs
   dedicated approved fixtures before it displays completed analyses.
5. **Keep conjunction-like discourse adverbs out of the main proof.** The
   current model has no agreed analysis for _however_, _therefore_, or
   _meanwhile_; none appears in the live corpus. A brief honest note may say
   that they link to prior discourse rather than act like _quickly_ in the
   controlled sentences. It must not call them `Conj` or silently assign them
   `supplement`.
6. **Build the optional-page infrastructure separately.** The page needs a
   route and data design that stays outside the numbered scope ladder, follows
   lesson 18, and has no practice gate or credit. That is an implementation task,
   not a change this research pass authorizes.

## Rejected

- **A fixed six-way or seven-way taxonomy.** Reference descriptions carve the
  semantic field at different places. The course can name useful labels without
  pretending that the list is a testable syntactic inventory.
- **Calling _twice_ an unqualified frequency adverb.** Its use in the proposed
  sentence counts occurrences; that is not the same claim as a habitual-frequency
  word such as _often_.
- **A single substitution, question, movement, or omission test.** Each can
  clarify a particular reading or structure. None produces a semantic class
  without interpretation, and none defines adverbhood in every construction.
- **The claim that meanings never affect syntax.** Meanings, position,
  attachment, and scope interact. The narrower, defensible claim is that this
  model does not encode a separate label for the four controlled meaning
  descriptions.
- **Folding conjunctive/discourse adverbs into `Conj` or `supplement` without a
  model decision.** Those labels would either misstate their syntactic role or
  hide their relation to earlier discourse.
