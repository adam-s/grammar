## Central generalization

An adjective before a noun is an **attributive adjective**: an adjective form
doing the premodifier job inside a nominal. It modifies the noun head, not the
larger noun phrase. In _the bright red kite_, _bright_ and _red_ narrow
_kite_; _the_ combines with the whole nominal _bright red kite_.

The job does not identify the word class. A noun can be a premodifier too: in
_the kitchen window_, _kitchen_ narrows _window_ in the same structural slot,
but it is a noun. The course should therefore ask two questions in order:
what job is the earlier dependent doing, and what form does it have?

`Nom` is already available from lesson 5. Lesson 16 does not introduce a new
phrase form; it supplies the first sustained reason to use the nominal layer:
the adjective and noun belong together below the determiner. The course's
analysis is `NP > Det + Nom`, then `Nom > premodifier(s) + N/head`.

## The grammatical relationships

| Example               | Form                        | Function                     | Relationship the diagram must show                                                                                          |
| --------------------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _the narrow gate_     | _narrow_: adjective         | premodifier                  | It describes or restricts the noun head _gate_.                                                                             |
| _the kitchen window_  | _kitchen_: noun             | premodifier                  | It specifies the kind or use of _window_, while remaining a noun.                                                           |
| _the bright red kite_ | _bright_, _red_: adjectives | two premodifiers             | Both modify the same noun head inside one nominal.                                                                          |
| _the poor_            | _poor_: adjective           | head, fused with premodifier | With no noun head expressed, the course records both the adjective's ordinary modifier role and the head role it now fills. |

The form/function division is the lesson's real content. **Attributive** names
an adjective's use before a noun; **premodifier** names the function, which is
wider than adjectives. A bare adjective such as _nervous_ after _seemed_ is
predicative, not a premodifier, even though it is still an adjective. Lesson 17
then takes up the boundary of an adjective phrase, which may itself fill a
premodifier slot.

### Stacked modifiers and the nominal layer

The current model makes _bright_, _red_, and _kite_ siblings inside one `Nom`.
That shows the relation learners need: both adjectives are dependents of the
same head, and the determiner has scope over the whole nominal. Some syntax
frameworks instead build repeated intermediate noun layers, especially to model
the order and grouping of several modifiers. That is a framework choice, not a
different word-class answer. The page should state the course's one-`Nom`
analysis without implying that every grammar text draws the same tree.

English does have usual semantic preferences in a string of attributive
adjectives: opinion commonly precedes size, age, colour, origin, material, and
purpose. _Bright red kite_ follows a natural preference. Those preferences are
about conventional ordering and meaning, however, not about whether a word is an
adjective or a premodifier, and they do not change this course's tree. They are
not a lesson-16 identification rule.

### The exceptional fused example

_The poor_ is useful evidence for the course's fusion feature, but it is not a
normal attributive adjective with the noun merely deleted. English often uses
_one/ones_ where an adjective is left without an overt noun, as in _the new
ones_. The collective construction _the poor_ has a restricted, conventional
meaning like “poor people.” Keep it after the core adjective-versus-noun
contrast, and do not let it support the false rule that _the + any adjective_
freely means “the adjective people.”

## Evidence and diagnostics

No single test defines the adjective class. Use converging evidence, and say
what each observation establishes.

| Evidence                                                                                  | What it supports                                                                      | Where it stops working                                                                                                                                                                |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Find the noun head, then inspect an earlier dependent that narrows it.                    | In this noun phrase, the dependent is a premodifier.                                  | Position does not decide form: it may be an adjective, noun, number, or a larger phrase.                                                                                              |
| Compare _the narrow gate_ with _the gate is narrow_.                                      | _narrow_ has a common adjective distribution: it is attributive and predicative.      | Some adjectives occur in only one position, and moving one may change its meaning: _former_, _main_, and the “alive” sense of _live_ are not evidence against their adjective status. |
| Try a degree or comparative form where the meaning permits it: _very narrow_, _narrower_. | Gradable adjectives often permit these forms; _kitchen_ does not in _kitchen window_. | Gradability is not the definition of adjective. Many adjectives use _more_ rather than _-er_, and many are not naturally gradable.                                                    |
| Replace a head noun plus modifiers with _one_: _the old red engine and the blue one_.     | The replacement can show that a noun-sized constituent includes an adjective.         | It does not prove the exact boundary or nesting of every modifier; it is weak evidence for the course's single-`Nom` tree.                                                            |

The best short procedure is therefore: find the noun head; identify what each
earlier word narrows; label that job **premodifier**; then use more than one
distributional fact to decide whether the word is an adjective or a noun. A
dictionary remains appropriate when those facts do not settle a lexical class.

## Common summaries, and what they omit

| Summary                                            | What it gets right                                          | What it leaves out                                                                                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “An adjective is a describing word.”               | Adjectives often express properties.                        | Meaning alone does not distinguish adjective from noun, determiner, or participle, and some adjectives restrict rather than describe.                                 |
| “An adjective comes before a noun.”                | This is the common English attributive position.            | Adjectives can be predicative or postpositive; some occur only in one position; nouns and other forms can also precede the head.                                      |
| “The word before the noun is the adjective.”       | It locates a useful candidate.                              | It mistakes position for word class. _Kitchen_ in _kitchen window_ is the live counterexample.                                                                        |
| “Put adjectives in opinion-size-age-colour order.” | It gives a useful editing convention for some long strings. | The categories are fuzzy, coordinate adjectives behave differently, noun modifiers enter the sequence, and no order rule identifies the course's structural relation. |
| “_The poor_ is an adjective used as a noun.”       | It notices that no ordinary noun head is expressed.         | It hides the course's fused head/premodifier analysis and overgeneralizes a restricted construction.                                                                  |

- `fix-nominal` shows _old_ and _red_ as adjective premodifiers of _engine_
  under `Nom`.
- `fix-noun-premodifier` puts _kitchen_ in the same function slot before
  _clock_, but with noun form.
- `fix-stacked` shows the separate `Nom` and `NP` layers over _old engines_.
- `fix-fused-premodifier` shows the exceptional fusion in _The poor

The ten practice sentences preserve strong composition across earlier clause
frames. Nine ordinary attributive-adjective instances occur in subject, object,
two-object, object-complement, linking, and required-adverbial frames. The set
also has exactly one stacked-adjective phrase (_the bright red kite_), one noun
premodifier (_the kitchen window_), and one fused case (_the poor_). Those three
sentences repair the two shortcuts identified in the previous dossier, but each
is still a singleton.

| ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

## Revision implications

1. Open with the form/function generalization, not “an adjective goes before a
   noun.” The first diagram should make the attachment visible: determiner over
   the nominal, adjective under it with the noun head.
2. Keep the existing adjective-versus-noun premodifier contrast as the main
   visual. Its caption must say that both words do the same job and differ in
   form. This is stronger evidence than a list of adjective tests.
3. Make the procedure explicitly two-stage: find the head and its
   premodifier(s), then identify form. Qualify the predicative and comparative
   checks beside the procedure rather than presenting them as definitions.
4. Keep _bright red kite_ as evidence that the premodifier function can repeat.
   Explain that the course draws both adjectives under one `Nom`; do not turn
   adjective-order mnemonics into a tree rule or a practice target.
5. Keep _the poor_ as a separate, late example of fusion, with the restriction
   above. It should not carry the central explanation and should not be offered
   as a deletion test.
6. In a later practice revision, add one controlled contrast such as _The
   narrow window rattled_ / _The kitchen window rattled_. Holding the head and
   clause frame still would make the form/function distinction testable rather
   than merely visible once. Do not add adjective phrases here: lesson 17 is the
   right place to extend a one-word adjective to an `AdjP` premodifier.
7. Preserve the current six-frame spread. It is valuable cumulative evidence
   that a noun-internal function is independent of the larger clause function.

## Sources actually opened and read

- [Cambridge Grammar, “Noun phrases: dependent
  words”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/noun-phrases-dependent-words),
  **English Grammar Today**, Cambridge University Press & Assessment. Read in
  full. It distinguishes determiners from premodifiers before the head and
  explicitly lists single adjectives, adjective phrases, single nouns, and noun
  phrases as premodifiers. Its noun-premodifier examples support the course's
  form/function contrast.
- [Cambridge Grammar, “Adjective phrases:
  position”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/adjective-phrases-position),
  **English Grammar Today**, Cambridge University Press & Assessment. Read in
  full. It distinguishes attributive from predicative use, records adjectives
  restricted to one position, and warns that position can change meaning. Those
  facts limit the linking-verb diagnostic.
- [Cambridge Grammar, “Adjectives:
  order”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/adjectives-order),
  **English Grammar Today**, Cambridge University Press & Assessment. Read in
  full. It gives the usual semantic ordering preferences and notes that the
  longest textbook-style strings are rare. This supports acknowledging order as
  a usage preference rather than a structural test.
- [Michael Rießler, _Adjective attribution_, chapter
  2](https://langsci-press.org/catalog/book/19), Language Science Press, 2016,
  pp. 5–12. Opened from the publisher and read from the downloadable PDF. It
  treats a noun phrase as headed by a noun or pronoun, treats adjectival
  attributes as dependent constituents, and uses English prenominal adjectives
  as a case where the dependency is shown by position. It also records the
  _one/ones_ pattern in English headless noun phrases. Its cross-linguistic
  framework is broader than this course, so it informs the limits rather than
  dictating the app's `Nom` notation.

## Rejected

- **Teaching adjective-order lists as the lesson's method.** The order is real
  usage knowledge, but it neither determines word class nor changes the
  modifier/head relation shown by the diagram.
- **Calling the word before a noun an adjective.** This abandons the course's
  form/function model and makes the noun-premodifier fixture inexplicable.
- **Using comparison or predicative position as a universal adjective test.**
  Both are useful evidence for _narrow_; neither survives the full adjective
  class.
