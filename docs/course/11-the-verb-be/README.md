# Lesson 11 — The verb _be_

Researched 30 August 2026. An author's dossier, not learner copy. See
[../01-introduction/README.md](../01-introduction/README.md).

**Page contract:** The learner-facing lesson is a static explanation under the
[shared lesson contract](../../lesson/README.md). This dossier records the
analysis, evidence, limitations, and revision brief that must control that page.

## What the lesson decides

| Decision | In plain words                              |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| `vt:Vbe` | this finite main-verb use is a form of _be_ |

`Vbe` is a **course label**, not a universally recognised extra verb type beside
intransitive, transitive, and linking. Reference grammars usually call the
relevant main-verb uses of _be_ copular or linking uses. The course gives them a
separate label because _be_ has exceptional forms and clause behaviour, and
because lessons 10 and 14 need to distinguish its two visible frames.

## Central finding

**A finite main-verb form of _be_ is exceptional in English, but the phrase
after it still determines the clause pattern.** In _The bread was stale_, the
phrase after _was_ predicates a property of the bread. In _The keys are on the
table_, the phrase locates the keys and completes the frame used in the course.
The app calls both uses `Vbe`; it does not call the location phrase a subject
complement.

The page should therefore teach two ideas in that order:

1. The relevant forms of _be_ have unusual grammatical behaviour: _am, is,
   are, was,_ and _were_ carry finite tense and agreement, and finite main
   _be_ inverts in a question without _do_.
2. Finding _be_ does **not** decide what follows it. A noun phrase or adjective
   phrase can be a subject complement; a required location or setting is a
   different function, taught formally in lesson 14.

This is a better reason for a separate course label than the page's current
claim that _be_ “supports more sentence frames than ordinary linking verbs.”
“More” is neither established nor needed. The point is that the course must
keep one unusually formed verb visible while it distinguishes two relationships
after that verb.

## The grammatical relationships

### A subject complement predicates something of the subject

In this live practice pair, _stale_ says something about the same bread named
by the subject:

> _The bread tasted stale._
>
> _The bread was stale._

The first verb is `Vlink`; the second is `Vbe`. The complement has the same
form and function in both clauses: an adjective phrase serving as a subject
complement. What changes is the verb, not the relationship between _bread_ and
_stale_. The same holds for noun-phrase complements such as _The winner was a
stranger_: the NP identifies or classifies the winner; it is not an object
acted on by _was_.

This is the useful bridge from lesson 10. It also prevents the course from
implying that a subject complement belongs only to ordinary linking verbs.

### A location phrase does a different job in this course

In _The keys are on the table_, _on the table_ supplies the location of the
keys. The course analyses that PP as an `adverbial` with `obligatory: true`:

> _The keys are on the table._ → _\*The keys are._

That is a framework choice worth stating accurately. School grammars commonly
say that _be_ normally has a complement, while reference grammars use several
analyses for locative and other predicative material. The course's
subject-complement/adverbial distinction is useful for the later removal test;
it should not be presented as the only terminology English grammar uses.

The location phrase does not name an identity or quality in the same direct way
as _stale_ or _a stranger_. It places the keys. This is why a page should not
describe every _be_ clause as simply “linking a subject to a description.”

### _Be_ has main-verb and auxiliary uses

The lexeme is broader than the lesson's `Vbe` label:

- main verb: _The bread **was** stale._
- progressive auxiliary: _The bread **is** cooling._
- passive auxiliary: _The bread **was** baked this morning._

The latter two constructions belong to the auxiliary and passive lessons. A
learner should not leave lesson 11 with the rule “any form of _be_ is `Vbe`.”
Within this lesson, the reliable scope is **a finite main-verb use of _be_, with
no following lexical verb**. The wider forms _be, being,_ and _been_ need not
be taught here, but the page must not imply that the five finite forms exhaust
the verb.

## Diagnostics, and what each one earns

### The controlled contrast

_The bread tasted stale_ / _The bread was stale_ is the best evidence available
to this lesson. It holds the subject and complement still, so the learner has to
notice the change from an ordinary linking verb to a form of _be_. It supports
the course's `Vlink`/`Vbe` distinction; it does not prove a general theory of
copular verbs.

### Question inversion

> _The keys are on the table._ → _Are the keys on the table?_

Finite main _be_ comes before the subject to make this yes–no question; it does
not need _do_. A typical lexical main verb needs do-support: _The keys remain
on the table_ → _Do the keys remain on the table?_

This is strong evidence for _be_'s exceptional behaviour, but it does **not**
find the `Vbe` label in a practice sentence. An auxiliary _be_ also inverts:
_Are the keys rusting?_ The diagnostic distinguishes a property of the verb
form, not a main-verb use from an auxiliary use.

### Form recognition

_Am, is, are, was,_ and _were_ are the finite forms relevant to the current
practice. Recognising one gives a learner a good first hypothesis, not a final
parse. The form may be an auxiliary in a later construction, and the phrase
after a main-verb use still has to be analysed from its relationship to the
subject and verb.

### Removal

Removal belongs to lesson 14, not to the definition of _be_. In the displayed
location example, removing the PP leaves the intended clause unfinished. That
shows why the course marks the PP required. It cannot establish that all
location phrases after _be_ are required, nor that every phrase necessary for
a sentence is the same grammatical kind.

## Common summaries and their limits

| Summary                                             | What it captures                                                                        | What it leaves out                                                                                         |
| --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “_Be_ is a linking verb.”                           | _Be_ often introduces a phrase predicated of the subject.                               | Its unusual forms and question behaviour; locative, existential, and auxiliary uses.                       |
| “_Be_ means identity or state.”                     | _She is a doctor_ and _the bread is stale_.                                             | Location, time, age, existence, and the grammar of the complement.                                         |
| “_Am, is, are, was,_ and _were_ are forms of _be_.” | The finite forms learners meet first.                                                   | _Be, being,_ and _been_, and the difference between main and auxiliary uses.                               |
| “_Be_ takes a complement.”                          | The Department for Education's useful school-grammar rule for ordinary copular clauses. | The course's separate treatment of required adverbials and the fact that terminology differs by framework. |
| “Use _be_ to make questions.”                       | _Is she your sister?_ needs no _do_.                                                    | Auxiliary _be_ behaves this way too; inversion does not identify a clause frame.                           |

The author-level summary is:

> _Be_ is an unusually inflected and syntactically exceptional verb. When it is
> the finite main verb, the phrase after it can predicate a quality or identity
> of the subject, or complete a locative frame; the course separates those
> relationships even while assigning the verb one `Vbe` label.

## Current evidence audit

The current practice set is much stronger than the earlier dossier reported.
It has nine `Vbe` readings and one `Vlink` reading:

| Reading                                | Practice sentences | What it makes visible                              |
| -------------------------------------- | -----------------: | -------------------------------------------------- |
| `Vbe` + adjective subject complement   |                  5 | A quality is predicated of the subject.            |
| `Vbe` + noun-phrase subject complement |                  4 | Identity or classification is not a direct object. |
| `Vlink` + adjective subject complement |                  1 | The exact _tasted stale / was stale_ contrast.     |

The `Vlink` entry, _The bread tasted stale_, is paired deliberately with _The
bread was stale_. This defeats the easiest shortcut: “an adjective at the end
means `Vbe`.” It is unusually good course evidence because the two sentences
keep every other decision stable.

The set does **not** put a required adverbial in a graded sentence. That is a
reasonable scope boundary: the formal adverbial labels do not arrive until
lesson 14. The page's `fix-vbe` fixture can preview the location frame in plain
language, but the page must make clear that lesson 14 supplies the diagnostic
and the label.

## What the research should change

1. **Lead with the matched bread pair, not the location frame.** It directly
   answers why `Vbe` and `Vlink` are distinct without forcing the reader to
   infer the comparison from different sentences.
2. **Replace “supports more sentence frames” with the central finding.** Name
   the exceptional finite forms and the two course relationships after the
   verb. Do not make an unmeasured comparison with “ordinary” linking verbs.
3. **Say “finite main _be_” in the inversion explanation.** State that
   auxiliary _be_ also inverts, and reserve progressive and passive analysis
   for later lessons.
4. **Keep _The keys are on the table_ as a preview, not the definition of all
   _be_ clauses.** Its PP has the course's required-adverbial analysis; lesson
   14 needs to explain and test that decision.
5. **List the form-function contrast explicitly.** Adjective and noun-phrase
   complements have different forms but the same subject-complement function.
   The practice set already earns this claim.
6. **Remove the claim that lesson 14 could not exist without _be_.** Other
   verbs can license required place complements. _Be_ makes a useful first
   example, not the whole phenomenon.

## Rejected

- **“_Be_ is just another linking verb.”** Adequate for an early school label,
  but it leaves out the exceptional finite forms that this lesson must explain
  and the course's required-adverbial frame.
- **“_Be_ is its own verb type in English.”** The app needs `Vbe`; English
  grammar does not have one uncontested inventory in which it is a separate
  type beside every other linking verb.
- **“Every form of _be_ is `Vbe`.”** Progressive and passive auxiliary uses
  are outside this lesson's frame.
- **“_Be_ always takes a subject complement.”** The location fixture is the
  course's immediate counterexample.
- **Using inversion as the test for `Vbe`.** It shows the exceptional behaviour
  of finite _be_, but it cannot distinguish main and auxiliary uses.
