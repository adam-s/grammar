## Central generalization

An auxiliary can make a grammatical contribution and also change what the
speaker is doing with the clause. `aux:modal` identifies the same structural
relationship whether _may_ marks a possibility or permission, and whether
_should_ gives advice or reports an expectation. The tree can show the modal
auxiliary; it cannot decide which of those meanings the speaker intended.

That boundary is narrower than “meaning is not grammar.” A modal's place in the
verb phrase, its bare-verb complement, its inversion, and its relation to
_not_ are grammatical facts. The unlabelled part is the choice among meanings
that context, speaker, audience, and the rest of the clause resolve.

Reference grammars often call an assessment of how likely a proposition is
**epistemic** modality and a rule, permission, or recommendation **deontic**
modality. Those are useful author terms, not two new kinds of auxiliary. The
same modal and the same syntax can support both, so a tree label would turn an
interpretation into a graded structural claim.

## What each helper contributes

Lesson 24 needs the structural distinction among helper kinds. This companion
needs to keep their semantic claims separate rather than treating them all as
versions of “time.”

| Construction                                      | What the model records                    | What it can contribute                                                                                             | What that does **not** settle                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _will approve_ / _may approve_ / _should approve_ | a modal auxiliary followed by a bare verb | a stance toward the event: prediction, possibility, permission, advice, obligation, ability, willingness, and more | one fixed meaning for the modal word, or future time for every _will_                                |
| _has approved_                                    | perfect auxiliary + past-participle verb  | a perfect construction, often connecting an earlier situation to a reference time                                  | that the event is simply “complete”; _has lived here since 2010_ can still describe an ongoing state |
| _is approving_                                    | progressive auxiliary + _-ing_ verb       | a progressive viewpoint, often focused on activity in progress or as temporary                                     | that the event literally continues at the moment of speaking                                         |
| _was approved_                                    | passive auxiliary + past-participle verb  | passive voice, which changes the clause's focus and can leave the agent unspoken                                   | why the writer chose that focus, or who the unspoken agent is                                        |
| _did approve_                                     | supporting _do_ + bare verb               | a place to carry tense, negation, inversion, and sometimes emphatic force                                          | a modal meaning; _did_ does not classify the event as possible or required                           |

The first finite helper normally bears the tense contrast in a chain, but the
course does not label tense. Core modals themselves do not inflect for a full
present/past/future tense system: _will_ can express a present belief or
willingness as well as a future prediction. A future time reading is therefore
not a safe definition of `aux:modal` or of _will_.

The contributions also stack. In _The plan may have been approved_, _may_
assesses the proposition, _have_ builds a perfect, and _been_ builds a passive.
The course can display all three helpers, but neither their order nor their
labels select one degree of certainty, identify the source of a rule, or reveal
whether the agent was intentionally omitted.

## The proposed demonstration, corrected

The present proposal says that its three sentences have identical trees. They
do not: _Those talks will resume_ is intransitive, while the other two contain a
direct object. They share the `Aux` / `auxiliary` / `aux:modal` path, not a whole
tree. Nor are the first two sentences built, so the page cannot yet show three
approved diagrams.

Use a controlled modal substitution once readings and fixtures are approved:

| Same structure                       | Plausible reading in context                       | What the diagram can establish                        |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| _The board will approve the plan._   | prediction, or a statement of willingness          | _will_ is a modal auxiliary before the same bare verb |
| _The board may approve the plan._    | possibility, or permission under a rule            | _may_ has the same structural job                     |
| _The board should approve the plan._ | a recommendation or requirement, or an expectation | _should_ has the same structural job                  |

These sentences hold the subject, bare verb, and direct object still. The three
approved diagrams would then really be the same tree except for the modal word.
Short contexts or captions can make two readings of the _may_ and _should_
sentences explicit, but they must not pretend to change the structure:

> “Under the statute, the board may approve the plan.”
>
> “After its earlier vote, the board may approve the plan.”

The first presents permission; the second presents a possibility. The completed
diagram stays the same. The point is not that the bare sentence has no meaning;
it is that the words alone can leave more than one ordinary reading available.

`c24-i` is usable only after its current gloss, “The board ought to pass the
plan,” is replaced or supplemented. That gloss selects the recommendation-like
reading before the optional page can show its ambiguity.

## Evidence, and where it stops

Two structural checks support the existing modal label:

- A core modal is followed by a bare verb: _may approve_, not _may approves_.
- It inverts and takes _not_ without _do_: _Should the board approve?_ and _The
  board should not approve_.

Those checks distinguish the construction. They do not distinguish prediction
from promise, possibility from permission, or advice from expectation. A
learner cannot use inversion, the word's position, or the bare verb to recover
one of those readings.

Contextual paraphrase is useful evidence, not a test. Ask whether the speaker
is assessing a possibility (“perhaps the board will approve”), granting or
withholding permission (“the rules allow the board to approve”), or
recommending an action (“the board ought to approve”). If two paraphrases fit,
the ambiguity is real. If only one fits after context is supplied, the context
has resolved the meaning; it has not created a different syntactic category.

Negation makes the limit especially clear. _Students may not park here_ can
withhold permission, while _The students may not park here_ can also mean that
perhaps they will not park. _You must not leave_ normally prohibits leaving;
_You do not have to leave_ says there is no obligation. The model can show an
auxiliary and a negative word. It cannot show which has wider semantic scope or
which social rule the speaker invokes.

## Common summaries and their omissions

| Summary                                                   | What it gets right                                                                         | What it leaves out                                                                                                                                                                 |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “Modal verbs express possibility or necessity.”           | Modals often express a speaker's assessment or a way of directing action.                  | Ability, willingness, requests, offers, promises, advice, prediction, and differences in strength; one modal commonly has more than one use.                                       |
| “_Will_ is the future tense.”                             | _Will_ often helps refer to a future event.                                                | It also expresses present belief, willingness, a promise, an offer, or a request; English has other ways to refer to the future.                                                   |
| “Perfect means completed; progressive means continuing.”  | Perfect and progressive constructions have visible forms and often produce those readings. | A perfect can describe an ongoing state, and a progressive can be habitual, temporary, planned, or interpreted through context. The forms do not provide a one-word meaning label. |
| “The auxiliary tells what the sentence means.”            | The auxiliary matters: removing or changing it can change the proposition sharply.         | The lexical verb, subject, negation, aspect, voice, and discourse can all change the reading. An auxiliary kind is not a complete semantic analysis.                               |
| “Every modal meaning deserves a different grammar label.” | The semantic distinctions are real and useful in close reading.                            | They are not new structural categories here: no general syntactic test selects them, and the same sentence can remain ambiguous.                                                   |

## Corpus and proposal shortcuts

There is no 24a practice set to audit because the lesson is not built. Its
proposal already gives away the intended meaning in the table's “The step”
column, so it would demonstrate rather than test even if it were made
interactive. That is appropriate for an optional static page, not for course
credit.

Lesson 24's built corpus also cannot support a semantic decision:

- `c24-a`, `c24-h`, and `c24-i` contain _will_, _may_, and _should_, but all
  three ask for the same `aux:modal` label. The learner can succeed by seeing a
  core modal before a bare verb; no sentence asks them to name its meaning.
- The glosses presently steer each modal toward one reading: _will_ toward a
  future paraphrase, _may have_ toward possibility, and _should_ toward
  obligation. They do not show the lexical item's range or the role of context.
- `c24-h` has a modal plus perfect auxiliary and `c24-i` is transitive, so the
  existing three modal examples are not a controlled same-tree set. A learner
  could infer that the word or its position, rather than the structural
  relation, is what the course tests.

This is not a defect in lesson 24. Its job is to classify an auxiliary
construction, and the structural checks are enough for that job. It is the
reason 24a must remain an ungraded companion rather than a hidden second
classification exercise.

## Revision and build implications

1. Keep `teaches` empty and do not add epistemic, deontic, permission,
   obligation, prediction, or scope labels to the palette. The point of the
   page is that these descriptions do not follow from a tree alone.
2. Build the companion only after two new parallel modal fixtures and their
   readings are accepted. The controlled _board ... approve the plan_ set is
   preferable to the current mixed intransitive/transitive proposal. It needs
   three static completed diagrams, not a question or a score.
3. Give the page one explicit ambiguity with two short contexts, and state that
   both contexts keep the same diagram. That is stronger evidence than a list
   of modal meanings or an isolated “Which does it mean?” prompt.
4. Qualify lesson 24's learner copy when it is revised. “Time, completion,
   continuation, possibility, or voice” is a useful first map, but it
   overstates simple one-to-one meanings: perfect is not always completion,
   progressive is not simply continuation, and _will_ is not simply future.
   Preserve the structural auxiliary lesson; link here for the semantic limit.
5. Retain _do_ in lesson 24's structural explanation. The current built
   examples include emphatic _did wait_ and _did file_, while its prose focuses
   on questions and negation. A later revision should say that support _do_
   can also add emphasis, without treating that as modal meaning.

## Rejected

- **Calling the current three sentences identical trees.** They share a modal
  relationship but differ in clause frame, and two have no approved parse.
- **A quiz that asks for one modal meaning.** It would either grade a context
  judgment or reveal the answer through the exercise wording.
- **“_Will_ means future” as the contrast.** It replaces one oversimplified
  label with another and hides present-belief, willingness, and speech-act uses.
- **Turning tense, aspect, voice, and modality into one semantic menu.** They
  are different grammatical systems that can occur in one auxiliary chain.
- **Saying meaning has no structural evidence.** It does: modal form, bare-verb
  selection, inversion, negation, and auxiliary order are real evidence. They
  simply do not settle the finer semantic label this companion discusses.
