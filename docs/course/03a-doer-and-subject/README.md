## Central generalization

A **subject** is a grammatical function: in this course's basic clause tree, it
is the `NP` paired with the predicate. A subject can refer to an intentional
actor, to someone who experiences a state, to something changed by an event, or
to no independently referential participant at all. It therefore does not name
one meaning role.

“Doer” is a useful first impression of the **agent** in many ordinary active
clauses. An agent normally acts intentionally. It is a prototype, not the
definition of a subject, and the question _Who or what does it?_ can identify
that meaning only after the structure is known. It cannot establish which noun
phrase is the subject in every clause.

This is defensible at lesson-3 scope as a warning about a tempting shortcut,
not as a procedure for finding subjects. The decisive structural cases arrive
later: passive voice puts a non-agent in subject position, and the model's
placeholder and displaced-subject analyses arrive with nominal clauses.

## The relationship the proposed diagrams would show

The three proposed sentences have the same outer course analysis:

```
S
├── NP / subject
└── VP / predicate
    └── V / head
```

| Proposed sentence       | What the subject means here                                    | What the matching tree establishes                          |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| _The audience clapped._ | a group acting intentionally; a clear agent or ordinary “doer” | It fills the subject function.                              |
| _The bridge collapsed._ | the thing that undergoes the change                            | It fills the same subject function without choosing to act. |
| _The rain eased._       | a weather phenomenon, not a chooser or intentional actor       | It fills the same subject function without being a doer.    |

The second row may be called a **theme** or **patient** in a particular role
system, but the boundary between those labels is not settled enough to teach
here. The third is deliberately not forced into a role name. The relevant fact
is simpler: neither subject is an intentional agent.

The diagrams would show a structural constant while the meaning relationship
changes. They do **not** show that a reader can identify a subject from meaning,
or that every non-agent subject has one shared role. That distinction matters:
the same `NP / subject` label does not make _bridge_, _rain_, and an
experiencer interchangeable in meaning.

An experiencer is an important omission from the current three-way contrast.
In _Mara heard thunder_, _Mara_ is aware of a perception but does not choose or
cause it. This makes the agent prototype narrower than “a person involved in an
event.” Add such a sentence only if its parse is approved and the page needs a
fourth diagram; it is not a reason to introduce a new learner label.

## Why the shortcut is real, and where it breaks

The course's live lesson-2 fixture _The rain stopped_ already has a non-agent
subject in the ordinary `S > NP/subject + VP/predicate` frame. Lessons 1–3 also
contain _The bell rang_, _The old gate creaked_, and _The kettle boiled over_.
Those examples weaken a deliberate-agent reading, but they do not make the
usual classroom question return a different noun phrase. A learner can still
answer “what stopped?” or “what rang?” and land on the subject.

The passive supplies the stronger later contrast:

> _The contractors dredged the harbour._
>
> _The harbour was dredged by the contractors._

In the first clause, _the contractors_ is the subject and the agent. In the
second, _the harbour_ is the subject but the contractors remain the agent in a
_by_ phrase. The active object has become the passive subject. Lesson 37 and
[lesson 37a](../37a-same-event/) are the right place to show that grammatical
change, because passive labels are not available at lesson 3.

Two other later patterns go further. Weather _it_ in _It is raining_ does not
refer to a weather-doer, and existential _there_ in _There is a problem_ does
not refer to a place or participant. The app analyses the latter as a
`placeholderSubject` with _a problem_ as the displaced subject; it reserves the
same placeholder analysis for extraposed _it_. Those labels first become
available in lesson 30, so neither construction can be a lesson-3 figure.

## Diagnostics and their limits

| Evidence or shortcut          | What it can support here                                                                              | Where it stops                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “Who or what chose to do it?” | An intentional agent in a plain active clause, as with _the audience_.                                | It asks about a semantic role, not a grammatical function. It misses themes, experiencers, weather subjects, and passives. |
| The matching three diagrams   | The course gives the same subject function to noun phrases with different relationships to the event. | It is an explanation of the model, not a test for a new sentence. All three proposed parses still need approval.           |
| Canonical statement position  | In lessons 1–3, the subject is the opening `NP` before the predicate.                                 | Questions, inversions, imperatives, passive clauses, and placeholders do not preserve that simple surface pattern.         |
| Subject–verb agreement        | It can support a structural analysis when number is visibly marked.                                   | The lesson-3 set is past tense, where its verbs do not show this contrast; agreement also does not encode agency.          |
| Active/passive comparison     | It shows that the same agent can be a subject in one clause and a _by_-phrase in another.             | It requires lesson-37 voice analysis and cannot become an early subject test.                                              |

The page should say plainly that no single test defines a subject. At this
point, a static contrast can keep the learner from mistaking a semantic hunch
for a structural rule; it cannot replace the lesson-2 boundary evidence or
anticipate later syntax.

## Common summaries, and what they omit

| Summary                                      | What it gets right                                        | What it leaves out                                                                                             |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “The subject is the doer.”                   | In many active clauses, the agent is the subject.         | Agency is a meaning role. Passive, experiencer, weather, and placeholder cases break the equivalence.          |
| “The subject is what the sentence is about.” | It can orient a reader in a simple statement.             | Topic is also a meaning or discourse notion; existential _there_ makes the sentence plainly not about _there_. |
| “The subject is the noun before the verb.”   | It describes the course's early declarative examples.     | It is position, not a definition; later questions and inversions change the order.                             |
| “Find who or what does the action.”          | It often points to an agent in an ordinary active clause. | It cannot distinguish an agent from another clause function and treats a useful prototype as a universal test. |

The Department for Education glossary itself calls the doer or be-er the
**normal** subject, then separately records its statement/question positions
and agreement. That is a reasonable school-level orientation. It does not
license the stronger equivalence that the current app hint implies.

## Corpus and proposal audit

There is no 3a practice corpus to audit. The optional page is a demonstration,
so it must not ask the learner to assign a semantic role or grade a choice. Its
only proposed visual shortcut is also its point: all three examples would have
the same top-level tree. That works only if the prose states the consequence —
the tree records the grammatical function, not the agent-like meaning.

The proposal has two evidence gaps:

1. All three exact readings are pending review and are absent from the built
   course. The page cannot render them yet.
2. The set has an agent and two non-agents, but no experiencer. It demonstrates
   that agent is not the only possible subject role; it does not support a
   lesson that lists every semantic role.

The built corpus has useful context but cannot silently stand in for the
proposal. `fix-sentence-frame` can support _The rain stopped_ as an approved
nearby example; it cannot certify the unbuilt _The rain eased_. Lesson 37's
active/passive pair is the later payoff, not an early worked example.

5. **Preserve the long arc.** Link forward to passive voice and 37a without

## Sources read

- Catherine Anderson, Bronwyn Bjorkman, Derek Denis, Julianne Doner, Margaret
  Grant, Nathan Sanders, and Ai Taniguchi, _Essentials of Linguistics_, 2nd ed.,
  [§7.6, “Events and thematic roles”](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/chapter/7-6-events-and-thematic-roles/), read in full.
  It distinguishes agent, causer, theme, and experiencer as roles in a verb's
  meaning; it also calls subject and object structural notions and uses a
  passive to show that the subject need not be the doer.
- The same textbook, [§6.11, “Changing argument structure: Causatives and
  passives”](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/chapter/adding-or-removing-arguments-causatives-and-passives/), read in full.
  Its passive analysis makes the active object the passive subject and demotes
  the active subject to an optional _by_ phrase.
- UK Department for Education, [_English glossary_](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/244216/English_Glossary.pdf), relevant entries read. Its entries for _subject_, _passive_,
  _object_, and _phrase_ support the school summary, agreement, statement and
  question positions, and the active/passive relation used by this course.
- Frank Robert Palmer, [“Passive,” _Grammatical Roles and
  Relations_](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/grammatical-roles-and-relations/passive/6A7A729220278115E3F0DA6A6CE8BCE4), accessible chapter summary read. It describes passives as promoting a patient or non-agent and demoting or deleting an agent. The full chapter requires access, so this source is supporting context, not a quotation source.

The thematic-role sources agree on the main distinction but not on one fixed
inventory or on every boundary between labels. That disagreement is a reason to
keep role names out of learner-facing labels, not a reason to hide the
subject/agent distinction.

## Rejected

- **“The subject is never the doer.”** False. The agent is very often the
  subject in active clauses; the lesson rejects an equivalence, not the useful
  prototype.
- **A semantic-role quiz.** The page should expose a distinction the app cannot
  fairly grade. It must not turn an arguable role label into course credit.
- **Using a passive as the main lesson-3 example.** It would be the clearest
  counterexample but would break the scope ladder. Lesson 37 owns the structure
  and lesson 37a owns the later comparison of one event in two voices.
- **Claiming that the proposed diagrams already exist.** They do not. Their
  accepted readings are a build prerequisite, not a detail to fill in after the
  page is written.
