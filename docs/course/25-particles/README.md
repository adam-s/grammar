## What the lesson decides

| Decision        | In plain words                                                        |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `form:Part`     | this word is the course's particle label, not the `P` that heads a PP |
| `func:particle` | it is a dependent in the verb phrase and belongs with the verb        |
| `part:verbal`   | it is the verbal kind of particle, as in _look up_ or _write down_    |

The label records the course's analysis of the word **in this construction**.
It does not say that spelling a word _up_, _off_, or _down_ makes it a particle
in every sentence. In _She looked up the number_, the course labels _up_
`Part/particle`: it is in the VP with _looked_, and _the number_ is the verb's
direct object. In _The children climbed up the ladder_, it labels _up_ `P` and
_up the ladder_ `PP`: _up_ heads the phrase and _the ladder_ is its complement.

That distinction carries lesson 19 forward. A `P` heads a PP and has the PP's
complement; a verbal `Part` does not head a PP with the following noun phrase.
The external attachment of _up the ladder_ is still a separate question: that
PP can depend on a verb in this example, but its `P + complement` structure is
inside the PP. Do not treat every word after a verb as either a particle or a
clause-level adverbial by position alone.

## Central generalization

A **verbal particle** is a verb-phrase dependent that forms a verb–particle
construction with the verb. In a separable transitive construction, the direct
object can come either after the particle or between the verb and particle:
_looked up the number_ / _looked the number up_. That alternation is strong
evidence that _up_ belongs with _looked_ and does not head a PP whose complement
is _the number_.

The construction need not be a one-word verb in the dictionary, and it need not
have an opaque meaning. _Look up_ is a conventional lexical unit in its
"search" sense, while literal _pick up_ remains transparent; both can have a
particle. Lexicalized meaning can affect which order speakers prefer, but it
does not replace the structural question of what the following noun phrase is
related to.

The inverse is not safe: failure to shift an object does **not** by itself prove
that a following word is a preposition. Some verb–particle combinations are
not freely separable, intransitive combinations have no object to test, and
object length, information structure, lexical choice, and variety all affect
the preferred order. This lesson should teach the positive evidence supplied by
an accepted shift and use the preposition contrast to show what that evidence
means.

## The grammatical relationships

| Relationship                  | What makes it true                                                                                                                                        | Approved example                                                                               |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Verb–particle relation        | `Part/particle` is a dependent of the VP alongside the verb and direct object; it is not the head of the object's PP.                                     | _She looked up the number_ (`fix-particle`)                                                    |
| Split and joined order        | A separable transitive verb–particle construction can place a noun-phrase object after the particle or between the verb and particle.                     | _She looked up the number_ / _She looked the number up_ (`fix-particle`, `fix-particle-shift`) |
| Pronoun order                 | An ordinary unstressed personal-pronoun object strongly favors the split order: _She looked it up_.                                                       | _The crowd cheered him on_ (`c25-e`)                                                           |
| P versus Part                 | A `P` heads a PP and keeps its complement; a verbal `Part` has no such complement in the course's analysis.                                               | _The children climbed up the ladder_ (`c25-i`) versus _She looked up the number_ (`c25-h`)     |
| Lexical meaning versus syntax | A verb plus particle can be transparent, conventionalized, or opaque. That semantic range does not decide whether the construction permits a split order. | literal _picked up the litter_ (`c25-a`); conventional _looked up the number_ (`c25-h`)        |
| Attachment                    | The particle attaches within the VP; a P first forms its PP with its complement, and that PP then attaches in the larger tree.                            | `fix-particle`; `c25-i`                                                                        |

The two existing fixtures are the right central contrast. They hold the words
and all decisions still except for linear order, and both show `Part/particle`
inside the VP. Their captions should say that the diagrams show the same
verb–particle analysis in two orders. They should not say that the particle
literally "moves" in the tree or that one order is the source of the other:
syntactic accounts disagree about whether either order is derived, and the
course has no need to choose.

## Evidence a learner can use, and its limits

| Check                                                                                                                 | What a positive result supports                                                                    | Limit that belongs beside it                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Try both orders with a noun-phrase direct object: _looked up the number_ / _looked the number up_.                    | The second order supports the verbal-particle analysis in this construction.                       | It applies only when there is a direct object and the particular construction allows separation. A failed shift is not a definition of `P`.                                                                        |
| Replace the direct object with an ordinary unstressed personal pronoun: _looked it up_.                               | The familiar split order is strong converging evidence for a separable verb–particle construction. | It is a near-categorical default, not an exceptionless law: focus, contrast, deictic force, and dialect can make joined pronoun orders possible. Do not build the lesson's definition on it.                       |
| Ask whether the following noun phrase is the complement of a P: _climbed [up the ladder]_ → _climbed up it_.          | The pronoun remains after _up_, supporting a P + complement analysis.                              | Pronoun substitution can make a sentence odd for meaning or usage reasons. Use a natural pair such as _up the ladder_ / _up it_; do not force a pronoun into _looked up the chimney_ and call the result decisive. |
| Compare an object shift: _looked the number up_ versus _\*climbed the ladder up_ in the intended directional reading. | The contrast distinguishes the course's two analyses of _up_.                                      | Another sentence may make the same word available in a different construction or with a different sense. Keep the intended reading explicit.                                                                       |
| Put a manner adverb after the full unit: _She looked the number up carefully_; _They climbed slowly up the ladder_.   | It can reinforce the different phrase boundaries.                                                  | Adverb placement is affected by meaning, rhythm, and dialect. The page's current pronoun sentence is a useful compact example, but its prose
overclaims twice. "A particle moves behind a pronoun object" sounds universal,
and the forced replacement of _the chimney_ with _it_ makes a semantic wrinkle
look syntactic. The revision should say that a neutral personal pronoun normally
goes between a separable verb and its particle, then contrast that pattern with
the natural PP _up the ladder_ / _up it_.

Fronting and stress should not be the lesson's tests. Preposition fronting and
stranding depend on question and relative-clause structure, while stress varies
with the phrase and speaker. Neither is needed when the course can show a clean
order contrast in its own parsed examples.

## Framework choice: particles and prepositions

Traditional learner grammars often call _up_ an adverb particle in _look up a
number_ and a preposition in _climb up the ladder_. That is a useful teaching
contrast and it is close to the app's `Part` versus `P` labels. Cambridge's
_Student's Introduction to English Grammar_ and _The Cambridge Grammar of the
English Language_ take a broader lexical-category view: many words called
particles are also prepositions, and **particle** names their role as a
complement in a verb–particle construction rather than a separate lexical part
of speech.

The course must state its own choice without pretending that the terminology is
uncontested. For Lesson 25, `Part` is the label for the verbal-particle role in
the VP; `P` is the label for a PP head with its complement. This keeps the
lesson readable, makes the two fixtures match the builder, and does not undo
lesson 19's nested-PP analysis. It also means that the lesson should not claim
that particles are "not prepositions" in every grammar, or that a bare spatial
word has only one possible category across frameworks.

The current set is better designed than the old research note reports, but it
has a genuine remaining limit: it repeats separable transitive constructions.
That is appropriate for a focused first lesson if the page says so. Do not add
an intransitive or an idiosyncratically inseparable case merely to make the
table broader unless the learner copy can explain why its main diagnostic no
longer runs.

## Revision implications

1. **Keep the existing fixture contrast as the main visualization.** It is a
   controlled alternation with accepted parses and uses only labels in scope.
   Change the wording from a claim that the particle "moves" to a claim that
   the construction permits both orders.
2. **Make the preposition contrast structurally visible.** Use `c25-i` beside
   a particle fixture only if the rendered diagram clearly shows _up the
   ladder_ as `PP` with `P/head` and `NP/complement`. If the page cannot show
   that relationship without exposing graded practice, add a dedicated approved
   fixture during revision rather than hand-drawing an answer.
3. **Replace the chimney pronoun test.** Use _looked it up_ alongside _climbed
   up it_, and identify the intended directional reading. Do not assert that a
   strange substitution supplies decisive grammatical evidence.
4. **Qualify pronoun placement and drop the word “definitive.”** Neutral
   personal pronouns are excellent evidence for this lesson's construction,
   but the research records exceptional joined orders and the test does not
   apply to every particle construction.
5. **Separate lexical unity from syntax.** Explain in one plain sentence that
   a familiar verb-plus-particle meaning can be stored as a multiword unit, but
   dictionary status and opacity do not themselves give the course label.
6. **Do not add stress or fronting procedures.** They create extra ways to fail
   and do not improve the available contrast. Keep attachment explicit instead:
   `Part` is in the VP; `P` first heads its PP.
7. **Preserve the practice sequence.** The switch and write pairs are useful
   controlled contrasts. Any future practice change should add evidence against
   the remaining _off/down_ word-list shortcut or an intransitive limit, not
   replace the existing P-versus-Part pairs.

The article was opened and read. Its 10,521-token study reports 99.8% split order for the relevant pronominal objects and documents the manual exclusions needed to keep PPs, passives, relatives, and string-search false positives out of a particle corpus.
- Barbara Lohse, John A. Hawkins, and Thomas Wasow, [“Domain Minimization in
  English Verb-Particle Constructions,” _Language_ 80 (2004), pp.
  238–261](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236791767_Domain_Minimization_in_English_Verb-Particle_Constructions), §§1–3. The article was opened and read. Its four-corpus study restricts its alternation data to constructions in which both orders are possible, excludes personal-pronoun objects because their order is fixed for that purpose, and finds that longer objects increasingly favor joined order.

This is supporting analysis, not the source of the course's label choice.

Cambridge English Grammar Today's learner-facing pages on phrasal and
prepositional verbs were located and their indexed text was reviewed, but direct
page opening returned HTTP 403. They are therefore not used here as the sole
support for any claim. They agree with the useful classroom contrast: separable
phrasal verbs permit two noun-object orders, while prepositional-verb objects
remain after the preposition.

## Rejected

- **Defining a particle as a short word that changes a verb's meaning.** It
  misses literal combinations, makes lexical opacity do syntactic work, and
  confuses a frequent property with the relation this lesson labels.
- **Calling particle shift the definition or “the definitive test.”** Positive
  alternation is excellent evidence in a separable transitive construction;
  failure has several other causes.
- **Treating pronoun shift as exceptionless.** It is the normal neutral pattern
  and a powerful teaching example, but the corpus evidence and focused uses
  require qualified wording.
- **Saying particles are categorically not prepositions.** That is a framework
  choice, not a universal grammatical fact. The course should teach its `Part`
  versus `P` parse and acknowledge the broader CGEL analysis in author-facing
  material.
- **Using raw corpus frequency as a parse.** Surface verb-plus-word searches
  cannot distinguish a particle from a PP, attachment, or an accidental string
  match without context and manual analysis.
