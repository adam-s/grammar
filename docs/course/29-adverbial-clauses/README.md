## Central generalization

An **adverbial clause** is a clause that relates the situation in one clause to
the situation in another: here, in time or as a reason. In the course model, a
subordinator such as _because_ or _before_ is a word inside that clause, with
the function `marker`; it is not a free-standing link between two sentences.

_The lamp flickered before the power failed_ and _Before the power failed, the
lamp flickered_ make the relationship visible. The words _before the power
failed_ contain their own subject–predicate frame and retain their marker in
both positions. They say when the flickering happened; they are not the subject,
object, or complement of _flickered_.

This is a function-first classroom label. Some grammars reserve **adverbial**
or **adjunct** for the function and use another name for the clause's internal
form; other teaching grammars call the whole construction an adverbial clause.
The app records both a clause kind and an `adverbial` function. That is a useful
course convention, but it is a framework choice, not a fact that the two labels
always name different natural kinds.

## What the live material shows

The ten practice sentences contain seven clause-final adverbial clauses and
three initial ones. Three pairs hold the relationship still while moving it:

| Final                                         | Initial                                        |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| _The lamp flickered before the power failed._ | _Before the power failed, the lamp flickered._ |
| _We waited because the gate was locked._      | _Because the gate was locked, we waited._      |
| _She waved when the last bus arrived._        | _When the last bus arrived, she waved._        |

The other four final examples add _until_, _whenever_, and _after_. All ten
subordinate clauses are finite: each has an overt subject and a tensed verb.
They express reason or time; none tests a condition or a concessive relation.
The outer clauses vary among `SV`, `SVO`, and `SVC`, but the inner clauses are
all simple subject–predicate frames, with one subject complement in _the gate
was locked_.

The two current teaching fixtures have separate jobs. `fix-adverbial-clause`
shows the final reason clause and the `Subord/marker` analysis. `fix-fronted-
adverbial-clause` shows an initial time clause and its comma. They are not a
controlled movement contrast: they change both the words and the relation.

## Form, attachment, and scope

The subordinator begins the dependent clause in every live example. That is why
_because the tide turned_ is one constituent, rather than _because_ connecting
two already complete sentences. A marker is evidence, not a definition: _after
the storm_ has the same first word but is a prepositional phrase, not a clause;
_that_ can mark a nominal clause in lesson 30 and a relative clause in lesson 31.

The whole adverbial clause has scope over an event or situation. In _The ferry
waited because the tide turned_, it supplies the reason for the waiting, not a
property of _ferry_ or _tide_. The same words at the front can guide how a
reader connects the two events, but fronting does not by itself establish a
unique syntactic attachment or discourse effect. Context can change whether a
reason is backgrounded, contrasted, or presented as the point of the sentence.

The current course trees make an implementation choice that the page must not
hide: final clauses are built inside the outer `VP`, while `whyFirst` puts an
initial clause directly under `S`. Yet each matched pair is intended to show the
same clause in another position. Before a revision calls that movement a proof,
the course needs to decide whether this is an intentional scope contrast or a
tree-building convenience. Either choice can be taught; the present lesson
cannot claim both that the structure is unchanged and that its parent changes
without explanation.

## Position and punctuation

Initial and final position are both ordinary for the relations in this set;
their availability differs by marker, relation, rhythm, and information
structure. _Until_ is especially common after the main clause, while the three
matched pairs show that time and reason clauses can be initial here. Do not turn
either tendency into a membership test.

In the current set, every initial clause is followed by a comma and every final
one has no comma. That is a useful pattern for the later punctuation lesson,
not a definition of an adverbial clause. Cambridge advises a comma after a
subordinate clause that comes first and not normally after one that comes
second. Editorial conventions still matter: Chicago explicitly treats the comma
after a short introductory adverbial _phrase_ as dependent on the risk of
misreading. Punctuation can support a boundary reading; it does not create the
clause or prove its function.

## Diagnostics, and what each one earns

| Evidence                                                      | What it supports here                                                                                         | Where it stops                                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Find an inner subject–predicate frame after the subordinator. | _the tide turned_ and _the power failed_ are clauses, not noun phrases.                                       | A marker may introduce a different kind of clause; a phrase after the same word may not be a clause at all.                                                         |
| Remove the whole clause: _The ferry waited._                  | The reason clause is an optional adverbial in this example.                                                   | It does not define every adverbial clause. Some clauses are selected or required by a head, and removal can also change what the speaker chooses to assert.         |
| Compare a matched pair and keep the marker with the clause.   | The three live pairs show that these particular clauses can occur initially or finally as a unit.             | Fronting is not a definitive constituency or category test: other constituents front, and some adverbial clauses resist fronting for semantic or discourse reasons. |
| Ask which event or situation the clause relates to.           | It distinguishes a clause giving a reason or time from a clause filling an object or subject-complement slot. | The answer can be ambiguous without context, and semantic relations do not fix a single tree by themselves.                                                         |

The old claim that the marker “cannot be dropped” should not survive. The
unmarked string _The ferry waited the tide turned_ is not standard edited prose,
but that fact does not identify a marker or distinguish every dependent clause.
English also has unmarked finite nominal clauses, as lesson 28 shows. The useful
claim is narrower: in these finite reason and time examples, the displayed
subordinator begins the subordinate clause and travels with it.

## Semantic relations are clues, not a closed taxonomy

School materials often sort adverbial clauses into time, place, manner, reason,
condition, purpose, result, and concession. The list can help a writer name a
clear relation, but the categories overlap and the same subordinator may have
more than one use. _Since_, _as_, and _while_, for example, can vary with
context. The syntax does not become different merely because a time relation is
renamed a reason relation.

Teach the relation carried by an example instead of making learners memorize a
fixed taxonomy. The current corpus earns “time” and “reason.” It does not earn
the learner page's broader promise of condition and contrast unless the source
or practice is extended with clear examples.

## Finite and nonfinite scope

Lesson 29 should work only with the finite constructions it actually shows.
Finiteness describes the verb form, while `adverbial` describes the clause's
job; they are separate answers, as lesson 28 established. A subordinator does
not guarantee a finite clause: _before leaving_, _although exhausted_, and _if
accepted_ are nonfinite subordinate constructions. Cambridge gives these as
ordinary possibilities after several subordinators.

Those constructions belong to the later infinitive and participial lessons,
where the course can address their missing or controlled subjects. This lesson
should neither label them prematurely nor imply that every adverbial clause has
the overt subject plus finite verb found in all ten current sentences.

## How common summaries help, and where they fail

| Summary                                               | What it gets right                                     | What it leaves out                                                                                              |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “A dependent clause that acts like an adverb.”        | Points toward the external relation to another clause. | _Acts like an adverb_ blurs a clause's form with its function and does not show attachment.                     |
| “A clause beginning with _because_, _when_, or _if_.” | Gives a fast way to spot many examples.                | Misses unmarked and nonfinite clauses, and mistakes _after the storm_ for a clause.                             |
| “A clause that tells when, why, or how.”              | Fits several ordinary uses.                            | Turns a flexible semantic range into a defining list and omits condition, concession, place, purpose, and more. |
| “A removable part of the sentence.”                   | Works for the current optional examples.               | Treats one adjunct diagnostic as a definition and ignores selected or required adverbials.                      |
| “A clause that can move to the front.”                | Fits the three matched pairs.                          | Is not unique to adverbials and fails for some otherwise good adverbial clauses.                                |

## What should change in revision

1. Keep the central claim on the relation between two situations, then use the
   _lamp/power_ pair as the controlled position contrast. Do not describe
   movement as definitive proof.
2. Replace the two unlike fixtures with a purpose-built matched final/initial
   fixture pair, or render the existing matched practice pair only if revealing
   that practice answer is acceptable. Its diagram must make the intended
   attachment consistent across positions.
3. Qualify the removal and punctuation tests beside the first use. Remove the
   claim that an omitted marker defines the construction.
4. Either narrow learner copy to time and reason or add practice that genuinely
   tests condition and concession. Add a phrase contrast such as _after the
   storm_ before calling a first-word scan a procedure.
5. Keep finite/nonfinite out of the learner procedure. A short forward link can
   say that later lessons handle reduced clauses such as _before leaving_.
6. Resolve the final-`VP` versus initial-`S` attachment decision before changing
   the explanatory diagrams or calling the placement pair the same tree.

## Rejected

- **A fixed taxonomy of meanings.** It is a memory aid, not the syntactic
  definition, and this corpus does not support its full list.
- **Fronting as decisive proof.** It is good converging evidence in the three
  matched pairs, not a test that identifies all and only adverbial clauses.
- **Comma hunting.** The punctuation pattern helps a writer read the current
  examples but does not distinguish the construction by itself.
- **Teaching nonfinite adverbial clauses now.** They are real, but extending
  this lesson to them would skip the later course's work on infinitive and
  participial clause form.
