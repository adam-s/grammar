| `kind:adverbial` | the clause says when, why or on what condition |

## The finding

**Every adverbial clause in the lesson comes after the main clause.** Ten out of
ten, verb-final ten out of ten.

Position is most of what makes an adverbial clause hard to find, and it is the
one thing the lesson holds constant. _Because the tide turned, the ferry
waited_ is the same clause doing the same job in the place a learner will
struggle with, and it does not appear.

Four distinct asked-shapes, which is respectable, and the marker inventory is
genuinely varied: _because, after, until, when, once, whenever_. That variety is
the lesson's strength.

## The tests

**Removal.** An adverbial clause is optional: _The ferry waited._ survives. This
is lesson 14's test at clause level, and unlike lesson 14 the lesson has
something for it to reject.

**Movement.** An adverbial clause can front. This is the definitive test that it
is an adverbial rather than a complement, and no sentence uses it.

**The marker cannot be dropped.** _\*The ferry waited the tide turned_ is not a
sentence. That separates `func:marker` from lesson 28's zero-marked nominal
clause, where the marker is optional.

## Shortcut register

| Shortcut                                | What defeats it                     | In the course?      |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| The dependent clause comes second       | a fronted one                       | **no** — 10/10      |
| A marker word means an adverbial clause | _that_ — lesson 30                  | **no** here         |
| The comma marks the boundary            | there is no comma in any of the ten | n/a — see lesson 39 |

The third row is the interesting one. A fronted adverbial clause is normally
followed by a comma, and lesson 39 exists to say punctuation is evidence.
Fronting a clause here would give lesson 39 something to work with, and lesson
39 currently has only one punctuation pattern.

## What this should change

1. **Front two clauses.** It is the missing half of the lesson and it feeds
   lesson 39.
2. **A matched pair.** _The lamp flickered before the power failed_ and _Before
   the power failed, the lamp flickered_ is the same clause in two places.
