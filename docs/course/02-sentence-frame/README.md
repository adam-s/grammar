## What this lesson teaches

Lesson 1 introduced the subject and predicate. Lesson 2 adds no new label. It
asks a harder question: **where does the subject end?**

Compare these sentences:

> The rain stopped.
>
> The shoes on my feet pinched.

The first subject is easy to see: _the rain_. The second contains another noun
phrase, _my feet_, which creates more than one place to cut. The whole subject
is _the shoes on my feet_.

That is enough to justify the lesson. A learner can know the words _subject_
and _predicate_ without being able to find their boundary in a longer sentence.

## Why the boundary gets harder

The College of San Mateo Writing Center warns that a subject becomes harder to
find when more than one noun comes before the verb. Its example is _The hole in
my shoes will have to be fixed._ The subject is not the nearby noun _shoes_. It
is the full phrase _the hole in my shoes_.

The same problem appears in this lesson:

> The hole in my shoes widened.

The hole widened. The shoes did not.

## The test: replace the whole opening group

Start with the first word and test where the subject might end. Replace that
whole opening group with _it_ or _they_.

> **The shoes** on my feet pinched. → \*They on my feet pinched.
>
> **The shoes on my feet** pinched. → They pinched.

The first cut leaves words stranded. The second preserves the sentence, so it
finds the subject boundary.

This is a test for a group of words, not a complete definition of a subject.
Other noun phrases can also be replaced by pronouns. The learner must test an
opening group and check the result against the sentence's verb. Agreement gives
useful second evidence:

> The key to the cabinets **is** missing.

The singular verb _is_ agrees with _key_, not with the nearer noun _cabinets_.

## The likely confusion

A short sentence makes weak shortcuts look like rules. Three are especially
tempting here:

| Shortcut | What defeats it |
| --- | --- |
| Cut before the last word. | _Our visitors arrived early._ |
| Choose the noun nearest the verb. | _The hole in my shoes widened._ |
| Expect every subject to have the same length. | _The rain stopped_ beside _The children in the yard shouted._ |

The better method is to test the whole opening group. Do not count words or
choose the nearest noun.

## Two names the learner may meet

School grammar often uses two terms for parts of a subject:

| This app | School grammar |
| --- | --- |
| the whole subject noun phrase | complete subject |
| the word at its center | simple subject |

In _the hole in my shoes_, the complete subject is all five words. The simple
subject is _hole_. This course does not ask the learner to find that central
word until lesson 5.

## What the learner page must show

The page should answer one question: _Where does the subject end?_

It needs:

- a short subject beside a longer one;
- a long subject that contains another noun;
- the replacement test, shown from a failed cut to a successful one;
- a contrast in which moving the boundary changes the meaning;
- a reminder that lesson 5 will open the subject and find its central word.

The current visual contrast uses the same words in two structures:

> **The workers in the tunnel** waited.
>
> **The workers** waited **in the tunnel**.

In the first sentence, _in the tunnel_ identifies the workers. In the second,
it tells where they waited. The boundary changes the relationship, and the
relationship changes the meaning.

## Scope

The two-part frame describes the complete statements used at this point in the
course. It is not a claim that every utterance has a visible subject and
predicate. Commands can leave the subject unspoken, and fragments are not
complete sentences. Those cases fall outside this lesson.

## Rejected

- **Another set built only from _The adjective noun verb_.** It changes the
  length of the subject without making its boundary harder to find.
- **The rule “ask who or what did it.”** It can help with these examples, but it
  confuses meaning with structure and fails on later sentence patterns.
- **A replacement test with no starting point.** Pronouns can replace noun
  phrases inside the subject too. The procedure works here because it tests the
  opening group against the main verb.
