# Lesson 2 — A subject and predicate give a sentence its basic shape

defines the page.

**Status:** Research complete. The live practice set is

## Central finding

In the ordinary finite statements used at this point in the course, a clause is
made by relating a subject noun phrase to a predicate verb phrase. The subject
does not merely supply a topic or a doer; it fills one role in the clause. The
predicate supplies the event, state, or relation in which that subject is
understood.

That relationship explains the role-switching contrast. In _The camera watched
the guard_, the camera is the watcher and the guard is part of the predicate as
the watched thing. In _The guard watched the camera_, the same two noun phrases
occupy different roles, so the claim changes. The lesson should make that
relationship visible before it asks a learner to find a boundary.

The title is deliberately narrower than a definition of every sentence. A
declarative statement can be evaluated as true or false, but a sentence can also
be a question, command, or exclamation. Those sentence types do not all make a
truth-evaluable claim, and English commands can leave the subject unspoken. This
lesson is about the canonical declarative clauses in its examples, not every
possible utterance or sentence type.

## The analysis this course uses

The app represents each worked example as this outer relationship:

| Structure | Function  | Job in this lesson                                           |
| --------- | --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `S`       | —         | the complete clause or sentence                              |
| `NP`      | subject   | one phrase in the clause frame                               |
| `VP`      | predicate | the verb phrase that expresses the event, state, or relation |

This is a structural analysis. **Subject** and **predicate** are functions; they
are not definitions of word classes and they do not name semantic roles such as
agent or topic. In the lesson's active examples, the subject is often both the
opening phrase and the participant that carries out or undergoes the event.
Those coincidences make a useful first example, but they are not the definition.

The course uses the familiar `S → NP VP` presentation for a first outer cut.
Other frameworks draw more layers above the verb phrase or reserve _predicate_
for a slightly different constituent. That disagreement does not affect the
local decision in this course: the `NP` marked **subject** and the `VP` marked
**predicate** are the two immediate parts of the canonical clause.

## What the current evidence shows

### The cited fixtures

The current page cites three approved fixtures, all displayed through lesson 2:

| Fixture                    | Sentence                        | What it can show                                           | What it cannot show          |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| `fix-sentence-frame`       | _The rain stopped._             | the outer `S` split into a subject `NP` and predicate `VP` | where a long subject ends    |
| `fix-camera-watched-guard` | _The camera watched the guard._ | the camera as subject and the rest as predicate            | a competing subject boundary |
| `fix-guard-watched-camera` | _The guard watched the camera._ | that role reversal changes the claim                       | a competing subject boundary |

The first fixture supports the page's basic claim. The two role-reversal
fixtures support the central relationship, but their current prose merely says
that the meaning changes. It must say _why_: exchanging the subject noun phrase
and the noun phrase inside the predicate changes who is related to whom.

None of the three diagrams shows the difficulty that the live practice set was
built to teach: an outer subject that contains another noun phrase. A revised
page needs a purpose-built, approved diagram such as _The hole in my shoes
widened._ Its top-level subject remains the full opening phrase even though
_shoes_ is another noun inside it.

### The live practice set

The ten live sentences are stronger than the page. Six contain a preposition
phrase inside the subject, with another noun phrase as that preposition's
object:

> _The shoes on my feet pinched._
>
> _The hole in my shoes widened._
>
> _A box of tools fell downstairs._

Four also continue after the verb, so a learner cannot always cut immediately
before the last word. _A box of tools fell downstairs_ combines both pressures:
there are two nouns before the verb and an extra word after it. The current set
therefore defeats the two shortcuts that the original corpus allowed: “the
subject is one word” and “cut before the last word.”

It does **not** yet defeat “the subject is the doer.” Every sentence has a
normal, overt, clause-initial subject, and none is passive or uses a placeholder
subject. That is an honest scope limit for an early lesson, not a reason to
teach agency as the definition of a subject.

## The boundary problem

Lesson 1 establishes the outer cut. Lesson 2 should make one consequence clear:
the subject can be a phrase, not just the noun nearest the predicate.

> _The hole in my shoes widened._

The phrase _in my shoes_ belongs with _hole_: it specifies which hole. The
embedded noun _shoes_ is not a second candidate for the outer subject; it is
inside the phrase that fills the subject position. This is a dependency and
constituency fact, not a fact about which noun is closest to the verb.

The visible contrast should hold the words as steady as possible:

> _The workers in the tunnel waited._
>
> _The workers waited in the tunnel._

In the first sentence, the location helps identify the workers and stays inside
the subject noun phrase. In the second, it locates the waiting and belongs in
the predicate. A revision can use this contrast only if an approved fixture
shows both attachments at lesson-2 scope. It teaches the subject boundary and
the reason the boundary matters: the two placements express different claims.

## Diagnostics, and their limits

No test defines a subject. The lesson should present any test as evidence for a
particular structure.

### Replacement identifies a phrase, not automatically a subject

In a canonical statement, replace a proposed opening run with a pronoun that
fits its number and reference:

> _The shoes on my feet pinched._ → _They pinched._
>
> _The shoes_ on my feet pinched. → \*_They on my feet pinched._

The successful version shows that the five-word run behaves as one phrase. The
failed version leaves part of that phrase behind. This is useful evidence for
the boundary in these examples.

It does not define a subject. Pronouns can replace noun phrases elsewhere in a
clause, and replacement requires changes of number, person, case, or wording in
many real sentences. The procedure is also restricted to the course's
canonical statements: a fronted phrase, a question, or an imperative does not
have the same simple “opening run” shape.

### Agreement can support the analysis later

In some finite clauses, the subject helps determine the form of the finite verb:

> _The key to the cabinets **is** missing._
>
> _The keys to the cabinet **are** missing._

Agreement is strong structural evidence when it is visible, but it is not
available in the lesson 2 practice set: its verbs are past-tense forms, which
do not show this singular/plural contrast. Lesson 5 uses this evidence to find
the head inside a subject phrase. Lesson 2 must not claim that learners can use
agreement on every sentence.

### Meaning checks the result; it does not supply the structure

Asking “who or what did it?” may help with _The hole in my shoes widened_, but
it confuses a useful interpretation with a structural definition. Passive
clauses, weather _it_, and existential _there_ separate the grammatical subject
from a doer, a topic, or even an independently referential participant. The
lesson's role-reversal contrast is better evidence: it makes clear that the two
halves contribute different roles to one claim.

## How common summaries help and mislead

| Summary a learner may know                                                          | What it captures                                          | What it leaves out                                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| “The subject is what the sentence is about; the predicate says something about it.” | The usual direction of a simple statement.                | A quantified subject need not name one thing, and the slogan gives no way to find a boundary.          |
| “The subject is the doer.”                                                          | Many active clauses put the doer in the subject position. | Passive, weather, and placeholder subjects break the equivalence.                                      |
| “A sentence has a subject and predicate.”                                           | The common declarative frame used in this lesson.         | Questions, commands, fragments, and multi-clause sentences need further analysis.                      |
| “Replace it with _it_ or _they_.”                                                   | A useful test for a noun-phrase constituent.              | The test does not tell the phrase's function, and it is not available unchanged in every construction. |

The current opener repeats the first summary and its closing repeats it almost
word for word. The revision needs one generalization that earns the page:
**the subject and predicate are the two clause functions that make the
relationship expressed by a basic statement; changing which phrase fills the
subject role changes the claim.**

## What should change in the learner lesson

1. **Replace the semantic slogan with the structural relation.** Open with a
   direct answer that says a basic statement relates a subject phrase to a
   predicate phrase. Keep “about” and “says something” only as a first
   orientation, not as definitions.
2. **Make the camera/guard contrast explain role assignment.** The current
   fixtures are useful, but the copy must name what has changed: who watches and
   who is watched.
3. **Add one long-subject diagram.** The page currently teaches a boundary with
   only one-word subject fixtures while practice tests a longer subject. Use an
   approved fixture based on _The hole in my shoes widened_ or an equally clear
   contrast, shown at lesson-2 scope.
4. **Give replacement as a limited boundary check.** Start with the proposed
   opening run, show a failed partial replacement and a successful full one,
   and say that the result identifies a phrase in this sentence. Lesson 4 can
   then generalize the test to noun phrases in other positions.
5. **State the scope beside the claim.** This page concerns the regular
   declarative clause pattern used in its examples. Do not say that every
   sentence has a visible subject and predicate or that every statement is made
   of one subject and one predicate.
6. **End on the generalization, not a restatement.** A suitable close explains
   that the subject-predicate boundary marks the roles that combine into one
   claim; it is why moving _in the tunnel_ or swapping _camera_ and _guard_
   changes what the sentence says.

## Rejected

- **“A sentence is defined by being true or false.”** Truth conditions belong
  to declarative claims; they do not define questions, commands, or
  exclamations.
- **“The subject is the doer” as the learner test.** It is a semantic shortcut
  that this practice set cannot yet refute and later lessons must undo.
- **“Delete every prepositional phrase.”** It can leave a useful smaller clause
  in a carefully selected example, but it erases different relationships in
  different places and cannot locate a subject boundary by itself.
- **A page made only of role reversal.** The camera/guard pair proves that
  grammatical roles affect meaning, but it does not teach the long-subject
  boundary that the live practice set asks learners to find.
