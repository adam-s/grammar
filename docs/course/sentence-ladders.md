# Sentence ladders for Course 1

Drafted 28 August 2026. Revised the same day against
[`sentence-review.md`](sentence-review.md), which found that most sentences could
be solved by position rather than by the test the lesson teaches. The revision
replaces sentences; it does not lengthen them.

This is the sentence-first plan for lessons 1–40. It is not lesson prose, an
answer key, or executable course data. The lesson writing comes later, after the
sentences have been reviewed, parsed, measured, and proved reachable.

Each lesson has ten sentences in three bands:

- **Plain, 1–3:** the lesson's work in its cleanest form.
- **Varied, 4–7:** the same decision in a new position, form, or familiar clause
  pattern.
- **Cumulative, 8–10:** the lesson's work combined with earlier work, one clear
  addition at a time.

The progression line is an authoring note. It names the intended climb without
giving the learner an analysis. A dagger (†) marks a sentence that deliberately
uses one of the missing Course 1 constructions identified in
[`difficulty.md`](difficulty.md). Those sentences require a fixture, scope
decision, or model check before they can enter the app.

Lessons 33 and 39 intentionally repeat two word sequences with different comma
patterns. Those controlled pairs isolate punctuation so it can be weighed as
evidence without changing the surrounding grammar.

These sentences are proposals. A natural sentence can still have a wrong parse,
and no automated audit can decide that. None should replace the current corpus
until a person has reviewed its reading and gloss.

## Stage 1 — See the frame

### Lesson 1 — Introduction

**Progression:** an unmistakable two-word frame, then a boundary that moves off
the end of the sentence. The predicate grows as often as the subject does, so
the cut can never be found by looking at the last word.

**Plain**

1. Birds sing.
2. The bell rang twice.
3. Those lanterns flickered all evening.

**Varied**

4. The old gate creaked in the wind.
5. My little brother sneezed.
6. The dog by the door barked.
7. Birds sang all through the evening.

**Cumulative**

8. The woman in blue smiled at the children.
9. Two birds on the fence scattered suddenly.
10. My little brother waited by the front door.

### Lesson 2 — A sentence has two parts

**Progression:** keep the subject easy while the predicate grows, then vary both
sides without introducing another clause.

**Plain**

1. The rain stopped.
2. The baby laughed.
3. Our visitors arrived early.

**Varied**

4. The kettle boiled on the stove.
5. A bright light flashed twice.
6. The children in the yard shouted.
7. The last bus left after midnight.

**Cumulative**

8. My neighbour's old bicycle broke yesterday.
9. The smallest puppy slept beside the basket.
10. Those heavy clouds drifted across the harbour.

### Lesson 3 — Find the main verb

**Progression:** begin with a bare verb, then place familiar material before and
after it so the tense-changing word must be found inside the predicate.

**Plain**

1. The tired visitors waited.
2. A small robin landed.
3. The candle suddenly sputtered.

**Varied**

4. The visitors quietly waited outside.
5. A robin landed on the rail.
6. The candle sputtered in the wind.
7. The loose window rattled all night.

**Cumulative**

8. The children near the fountain played quietly.
9. A line of trucks moved through town.
10. The smallest boat in the harbour returned safely.

### Lesson 4 — Noun phrases

**Progression:** replace a short subject as one unit, then preserve that unit as
modifiers are added before and after its central noun. The predicate carries
words too, so the noun phrase is not simply everything before the last word.

**Plain**

1. The engine suddenly stalled.
2. Those high windows rattled.
3. My quiet neighbour waved.

**Varied**

4. The old engine stalled again.
5. Those upstairs windows rattled in the wind.
6. My neighbour from Leeds waved at us.
7. The parcel by the stairs disappeared.

**Cumulative**

8. The old engine near the gate stalled twice.
9. Those narrow windows above the sink rattled all night.
10. My quiet neighbour from across the road waved.

### Lesson 5 — Find the head

**Progression:** start with one clear noun inside the subject, then add material
that depends on it. From item 6 the head stops being the last noun before the
verb, so counting position cannot find it. The final item introduces a flat
name, whose nouns do not choose a single head.

**Plain**

1. The clock stopped.
2. A branch snapped.
3. Those children cheered.

**Varied**

4. The kitchen clock stopped.
5. A heavy branch snapped.
6. The clock near the door stopped.
7. A box of tools fell.

**Cumulative**

8. The heavy branch above the path snapped.
9. The key to the cabinet vanished.
10. New York glittered at night. †

### Lesson 6 — Determiners

**Progression:** articles, demonstratives, possessives, and quantities all fill
the same place. The last three expose the boundary cases the current corpus
omits: a determiner with no noun after it, a determinative phrase, and a
possessive noun phrase doing a determiner's job.

**Plain**

1. The brass bell rang.
2. A window opened.
3. Those dogs barked.

**Varied**

4. My phone buzzed loudly.
5. Every seat squeaked again.
6. Several boats returned to the harbour.
7. No lights remained.

**Cumulative**

8. Most agreed. †
9. Almost every seat squeaked all evening. †
10. The driver's phone buzzed. †

### Lesson 7 — Pronouns

**Progression:** familiar personal pronouns, then number, reference, and
indefinite pronouns, all filling the complete subject noun-phrase slot.

**Plain**

1. She paused briefly.
2. They quietly agreed.
3. It vanished completely.

**Varied**

4. We waited outside the station.
5. He returned at noon.
6. Someone knocked twice.
7. Nothing moved upstairs.

**Cumulative**

8. Both arrived before sunset.
9. Several stayed near the entrance.
10. Nobody in the back row complained.

## Stage 2 — Let the verb predict the clause

### Lesson 8 — Verbs that stand alone

**Progression:** clear physical events, then less visible events and optional
surrounding detail. None of the verbs needs another participant to complete it.

**Plain**

1. The sleepy baby rested.
2. Our tired guests arrived.
3. The old engine failed.

**Varied**

4. She laughed very loudly.
5. Several candles flickered nearby.
6. The old bridge collapsed overnight.
7. Nobody objected during the meeting.

**Cumulative**

8. Those tired runners rested beside the track.
9. The last train from Bristol arrived late.
10. A flock of geese settled beyond the field.

### Lesson 9 — Verbs that take an object

**Progression:** a clear “verb what?” answer, then objects that vary in
determiner, number, and internal size while the transitive frame stays stable.
The last two are the same verb with and without an object, so the frame stops
being given by the lesson and has to be tested.

**Plain**

1. The mechanic checked the brakes.
2. She quietly opened the parcel.
3. Our dog chased a squirrel.

**Varied**

4. The clerk copied those letters.
5. We carried those heavy boxes.
6. A strong gust moved the chairs.
7. The young painter cleaned every brush.

**Cumulative**

8. My neighbour repaired the broken gate.
9. The gate opened.
10. She opened the gate.

### Lesson 10 — Linking verbs

**Progression:** adjective complements first, then noun-phrase complements, then
the same verbs in their other senses. Items 8, 9 and 10 reuse _felt_ and _grew_
transitively and intransitively, so the verb has to be tested rather than
recognised.

**Plain**

1. The hot soup tasted salty.
2. The evening sky grew dark.
3. Her careful plan seemed sensible.

**Varied**

4. The rough cloth felt unpleasant.
5. Those ripe berries turned purple.
6. My uncle became a teacher.
7. The quiet visitor seemed our best guide.

**Cumulative**

8. She felt the rough cloth.
9. The farmer grew potatoes.
10. The children grew.

### Lesson 11 — The verb _be_

**Progression:** distinguish _be_ from other linking verbs while its complement
moves from a plain description to an identifying noun phrase. The last two hold
everything but the verb steady.

**Plain**

1. The small room was quiet.
2. Those leather shoes are wet.
3. Her final answer was correct.

**Varied**

4. My sister is a pilot.
5. The tall building was a school.
6. These berries are unusually sweet.
7. The boat in the harbour was ours.

**Cumulative**

8. Our new neighbour is the town librarian.
9. The hot soup tasted salty.
10. The hot soup was salty.

### Lesson 12 — Two objects

**Progression:** establish receiver before thing, then vary each object and mix
in earlier one-object frames so the second object must be predicted rather than
counted. The last item is the same event with a preposition, which is one object
and an adverbial.

**Plain**

1. The guide gave us a map.
2. She sent her brother a note.
3. Our host offered the guests tea.

**Varied**

4. The clerk handed me those forms.
5. We showed the children our garden.
6. The coach taught every player the routine.
7. My aunt bought her neighbour a warm coat.

**Cumulative**

8. The porter carried the heavy cases.
9. The porter brought our tired guests their cases.
10. The guide gave a map to us.

### Lesson 13 — Naming the object

**Progression:** plain adjective complements of the object, then noun-phrase
complements, followed by contrasts with transitive and linking frames.

**Plain**

1. The jurors found the driver careless.
2. We painted the door blue.
3. The news made everyone anxious.

**Varied**

4. They elected young Mara captain.
5. The children named the puppy Rocket.
6. The cold weather left the roads dangerous.
7. Her clear explanation made the problem simple.

**Cumulative**

8. The committee chose the quiet room.
9. The committee considered the quiet room suitable.
10. The committee declared that small room the winner.

### Lesson 14 — When an adverbial is required

**Progression:** obligatory place after _be_, then verbs that require a place
with and without an object, followed by matched optional adverbials that survive
the removal test.

**Plain**

1. The spare keys are on the desk.
2. The meeting lasted nearly two hours.
3. She put the milk in the fridge.

**Varied**

4. The path leads through the woods.
5. We placed every chair near the wall.
6. The tired children stayed safely outside.
7. The driver kept the engine at the depot.

**Cumulative**

8. The tired children played happily outside.
9. The careful driver checked the engine at the depot.
10. Our guests put their wet coats beside the warm radiator.

### Lesson 15 — The six types, one procedure

**Progression:** revisit the simple frames in the order the verb adds demands,
then hold the shape completely still. Items 8 and 9 are the same five words with
different verbs and different types; item 10 has to survive the removal test
that item 2 does not need.

**Plain**

1. The whole audience laughed loudly.
2. The caretaker locked the gate.
3. The hot soup smelled wonderful.

**Varied**

4. My neighbour is a carpenter.
5. The nurse gave the child some water.
6. The board appointed Lena treasurer.
7. The spare keys are in the drawer.

**Cumulative**

8. They called her a taxi.
9. They called her a genius.
10. She kept the milk in the fridge.

## Stage 3 — Build phrases from the inside out

### Lesson 16 — Adjectives before nouns

**Progression:** one adjective beside its noun, then the same structure in
different clause slots, then adjective phrases and post-head material. The last
sentence adds the missing fused-adjective construction.

**Plain**

1. The bright red kite fell.
2. A narrow wooden bridge shook.
3. The mechanic repaired the old engine.

**Varied**

4. Those noisy children woke the baby.
5. The careful nurse closed every open window.
6. A very strong wind damaged the wooden fence.
7. My youngest cousin found the missing key.

**Cumulative**

8. The old lock on the shed rusted.
9. The unusually quiet child near the door smiled.
10. The poor near the town hall protested outside. †

### Lesson 17 — Adjective phrases

**Progression:** a one-word adjective phrase, then a degree word, then a
complement after the adjective. The cumulative items place expanded adjective
phrases inside noun phrases and linking clauses.

**Plain**

1. The lake water felt cold.
2. The water felt very cold.
3. The box seemed too heavy.

**Varied**

4. The children were eager to help.
5. My neighbour seemed proud of her garden.
6. We found the instructions unusually clear.
7. The road became narrow near the bridge.

**Cumulative**

8. The extremely narrow road crossed the marsh.
9. Our proud new captain thanked every player.
10. The children seemed remarkably eager to help.

### Lesson 18 — Adverbs and adverb phrases

**Progression:** optional one-word adverbials, then changes in position and
degree. The final three move the adverb rather than lengthen it: one expanded
adverb phrase, one adverb between the subject and the verb, and one inside an
adjective phrase.

**Plain**

1. The baby slept peacefully.
2. Our visitors arrived unusually early.
3. The engine started slowly.

**Varied**

4. Yesterday the children played outside.
5. The old clock ticked surprisingly loudly.
6. She answered the difficult question quite calmly.
7. The smallest boat moved unusually quickly.

**Cumulative**

8. Our tired guests walked home very slowly. †
9. The children quietly left.
10. The road was surprisingly narrow.

### Lesson 19 — Prepositional phrases

**Progression:** recall a preposition with a noun-phrase complement, then let a
second preposition expand that complement. This is the one lesson whose content
is depth, so it should be the longest in its stage. The deep items nest inside a
noun phrase, where only one attachment is available; stacking after a verb is
lesson 27's work.

**Plain**

1. The cat slept quietly under the table.
2. Our guests waited patiently by the gate.
3. She placed the lamp on the shelf.

**Varied**

4. The cat crept out from under the table.
5. A voice came from behind the wall.
6. We walked along the path beside the river.
7. We found the key to the door of the shed.

**Cumulative**

8. The nervous cat hid deep inside the cupboard.
9. Our youngest guest waited just outside the room.
10. The lid of the box of old tools cracked.

### Lesson 20 — Form is not function

**Progression:** hold the prepositional-phrase form steady while its job changes
between required and optional adverbial or complement inside another phrase.
Matched wording makes the changed job easier to hear.

**Plain**

1. The spare keys are on the table.
2. The children worked on the table.
3. The lamp stood beside the window.

**Varied**

4. The painter worked beside the window.
5. She placed the box under the bench.
6. She opened the box under the bench.
7. A sound came from behind the curtain.

**Cumulative**

8. The curious child listened from behind the curtain.
9. Our guide put the heavy bags inside the cupboard.
10. The patient guide waited inside the cupboard during the game.

### Lesson 21 — Modifiers after the head

**Progression:** a short phrase after a noun, then postmodifiers in subject and
object positions. Earlier premodifiers enter before the postmodifier and remain
through the cumulative band.

**Plain**

1. The map on the shelf fell.
2. A child with a drum waved.
3. She repaired the gate by the road.

**Varied**

4. The old book on the shelf fell yesterday.
5. A cheerful child with a red drum waved.
6. The mechanic repaired the rusty gate by the road.
7. We followed the narrow path through the woods.

**Cumulative**

8. The old lock on the garden shed rusted badly.
9. Those careful workers from the repair shop checked every window.
10. My cousin with the red scarf carried the parcel.

### Lesson 22 — Appositives

**Progression:** rename a subject, then an object, then place fuller noun phrases
on either side of the appositive. Items 8 and 9 are the same four words with and
without commas, so the relation cannot be found by hunting for punctuation.

**Plain**

1. Lena, our team captain, spoke.
2. The pilot, young Mara, waved.
3. We thanked Arun, our patient guide.

**Varied**

4. My neighbour, a carpenter, repaired the gate.
5. The smallest boat, a wooden dinghy, drifted away.
6. The children named the puppy, a sleepy spaniel, Rocket.
7. Our host showed me the attic, a narrow room.

**Cumulative**

8. Our guide Arun waved. †
9. Our guide, Arun, waved.
10. We gave Arun, our village guide, the spare map.

### Lesson 23 — Numbers in noun phrases

**Progression:** cardinal numbers determine a noun, ordinal numbers premodify
one, and then the two patterns combine with earlier noun-phrase structure. The
last item makes the number itself the head.

**Plain**

1. Three brass bells rang.
2. The first train arrived.
3. She packed four boxes.

**Varied**

4. Those two windows rattled.
5. The second narrow bridge collapsed.
6. We gave five guests a map.
7. The first two runners crossed the line.

**Cumulative**

8. Three old boats near the pier returned.
9. The last four library volunteers packed every book.
10. Those final three remained outside. †

### Lesson 24 — Auxiliary verbs

**Progression:** one clear example of each non-passive auxiliary job, then
contrasts and short chains. The main verb remains identifiable while tense or
aspect moves onto a helper.

**Plain**

1. The visitors will wait.
2. The visitors have waited.
3. The visitors are waiting.

**Varied**

4. The visitors did wait.
5. She can repair the gate.
6. She has repaired the gate.
7. She is repairing the gate.

**Cumulative**

8. The careful mechanic may have checked the brakes.
9. Our youngest guests have been waiting outside.
10. Those volunteers will be packing the library books.

### Lesson 25 — Particles

**Progression:** unmistakable verb particles, then the same surface words used
as prepositions. Items 1 and 2, 4 and 5, and 10 move the particle behind the
object, which a preposition cannot do. Items 6 and 7, and 8 and 9, hold the
words still and change only what follows.

**Plain**

1. She switched off the lamp.
2. She switched the lamp off.
3. The crowd cheered him on.

**Varied**

4. We wrote down the address.
5. We wrote the address down.
6. The children picked up the litter.
7. The children climbed up the ladder.

**Cumulative**

8. Mara looked over the report.
9. Mara looked over the fence.
10. The careful clerk took every name down.

### Lesson 26 — Coordination inside phrases

**Progression:** coordinate plain noun phrases, then adjectives and
prepositional phrases, followed by larger phrases whose internal structures have
already been learned. The last item is a correlative, which pairs a marker at
the front with the coordinator.

**Plain**

1. The cats and the dogs slept quietly.
2. We packed the books and the maps.
3. The flag was bright red and gold.

**Varied**

4. The children ran through the gate and across the field.
5. Our calm and patient guide explained the route.
6. The small boat carried food, water, and blankets.
7. She placed the lamp on the desk or beside the window.

**Cumulative**

8. The old clock and the brass lamp disappeared.
9. Our careful volunteers packed every book and map.
10. Both the clock and the lamp disappeared. †

### Lesson 27 — Attachment changes meaning

**Progression:** begin with familiar object-versus-verb attachment, then make
both possible readings carry richer noun phrases. The last item is a different
ambiguity: how far the adjective reaches across the coordinator. Every item must
retain two ordinary, defensible readings after human review.

**Plain**

1. I saw the guard with the binoculars.
2. She photographed the child beside the fountain.
3. We discussed the plan with the architect.

**Varied**

4. The reporter interviewed the actor in the garden.
5. Mara found the letter under the cushion.
6. The teacher greeted the student with the red scarf.
7. Our neighbour watched the dog near the gate.

**Cumulative**

8. The careful inspector examined the pipe with the small camera.
9. The village reporter interviewed the driver beside the bus.
10. The guide met the old men and women.

## Stage 4 — Put clauses inside clauses

### Lesson 28 — Main and dependent clauses

**Progression:** a short unmarked clause in the object slot, then an explicit
marker, then a dependent clause whose own frame grows while the outer clause
stays easy to find.

**Plain**

1. She knew the side door opened.
2. The nurse thought the baby slept.
3. The guide said the bridge was safe.

**Varied**

4. She knew that the door opened.
5. We heard that the baby had cried.
6. The guide said that the narrow bridge was safe.
7. Our neighbour believed the children found the key.

**Cumulative**

8. The careful mechanic reported that the old engine had failed.
9. Our guide explained that the woodland path was closed.
10. The visitors discovered that their last train had left.

### Lesson 29 — Adverbial clauses

**Progression:** one marked reason or time clause after a simple main clause,
then different semantic relations and positions. Later items retain phrase work
inside both clauses. Item 10 is item 5 with the clause moved to the front.

**Plain**

1. We waited because the gate was locked.
2. She waved when the last bus arrived.
3. The match ended after the rain began.

**Varied**

4. The children stayed inside although the sun was shining.
5. The lamp flickered before the power failed.
6. Because the gate was locked, we waited outside.
7. When the last bus arrived, our guests gathered their bags.

**Cumulative**

8. The careful driver slowed down because the narrow road was icy.
9. Although the engine failed, the boat reached harbour.
10. Before the power failed, the lamp flickered.

### Lesson 30 — Nominal clauses

**Progression:** recall a nominal clause as object, then move the same form into
the subject slot. Later sentences enlarge the clause internally without changing
its noun-like job. Item 9 puts one in a subject-complement slot, and the final
item introduces the missing fused-relative shape.

**Plain**

1. She knew that the gate was open.
2. That the gate was open surprised her.
3. That the baby slept relieved us.

**Varied**

4. We believed that the old bridge was safe.
5. That the old bridge was safe pleased the guide.
6. That every window rattled worried my neighbour.
7. That the visitors had missed their train became obvious.

**Cumulative**

8. That the careful mechanic repaired the engine impressed the driver.
9. The trouble was that the gate was locked.
10. What the children wanted surprised their patient teacher. †

### Lesson 31 — Relative clauses

**Progression:** a relative with a missing subject, then the same modifier in
subject and object noun phrases. Object-gap relatives enter only after the
subject-gap pattern is stable. Item 5 is item 2 with commas, item 8 is item 6
with the relative word taken out, and item 9 uses a relative that is itself a
determiner.

**Plain**

1. The cheerful child who waved smiled again.
2. The old engine that failed was replaced.
3. We thanked the patient guide who waited.

**Varied**

4. The child who carried the red flag smiled.
5. The old engine, which failed, was replaced. †
6. The borrowed book that I needed disappeared.
7. She repaired the gate that the storm damaged.

**Cumulative**

8. The book I needed disappeared.
9. The child whose red flag fell smiled. †
10. The boat that the volunteers repaired reached harbour.

### Lesson 32 — Comparative clauses

**Progression:** a short comparison with one missing repeated element, then
comparisons inside richer adjective phrases and clause frames.

**Plain**

1. The heavy box was heavier than we expected.
2. The woodland path was longer than it looked.
3. She arrived much earlier than I had predicted.

**Varied**

4. The new engine ran more quietly than the old one did.
5. Our youngest guest was taller than we remembered.
6. The repair cost less than the mechanic estimated.
7. The river rose faster than the volunteers could move.

**Cumulative**

8. The woodland path was steeper than our guide had warned.
9. The carefully repaired engine sounded much quieter than the driver expected.
10. The volunteers who repaired the boat worked faster than we expected.

### Lesson 33 — Coordination between clauses

**Progression:** join two plain independent clauses, then coordinate clauses
with different familiar frames. The final items carry embedding and modifiers
on both sides without omitting repeated material.

**Plain**

1. The brass bell rang and the heavy doors opened.
2. She waved but nobody in the crowd answered.
3. We can wait here or we can leave now.

**Varied**

4. The rain stopped and the children ran outside.
5. The mechanic checked the brakes but the driver heard another noise.
6. Our guide was calm and the narrow path seemed safe.
7. She gave us a map but we chose the wrong road.

**Cumulative**

8. The visitors knew the gate was closing but they waited.
9. The flag bearer smiled and our guide waved back.
10. The engine ran quietly but the driver still looked worried.

## Stage 5 — Handle reduced and marked structures

### Lesson 34 — Infinitive clauses

**Progression:** a plain infinitive as the complement of a verb, then an overt
subject and an object inside the infinitive. The last item places the infinitive
inside an adjective phrase, a missing Course 1 construction.

**Plain**

1. She sincerely hoped to leave soon.
2. We finally decided to wait outside.
3. The tired children wanted to help us.

**Varied**

4. She hoped to catch the last train.
5. We asked the driver to wait.
6. The guide expected the visitors to arrive early.
7. Our neighbours planned to repair the old gate.

**Cumulative**

8. The child who found the key promised to return it.
9. Our guide asked the visitors to wait outside.
10. Those boxes were too heavy to lift. †

### Lesson 35 — Participial clauses

**Progression:** a reduced modifier with a clear missing object, then fuller
participial clauses after nouns in different clause positions. Items 8 and 9 are
the two shapes neither corpus has ever held: a present participle after its
noun, and a participial clause at the front of the sentence. Every sentence must
survive an ordinary-speech check before parsing.

**Plain**

1. The window broken by the storm rattled.
2. The letter written in haste confused us.
3. We crossed the bridge damaged by the flood.

**Varied**

4. The engine repaired early yesterday started easily.
5. She returned the book borrowed from Mara.
6. The food prepared by our neighbours smelled wonderful.
7. We followed the path marked with yellow arrows.

**Cumulative**

8. The child standing by the gate waved. †
9. Damaged by the flood, the bridge closed. †
10. The map drawn by our woodland guide proved accurate.

### Lesson 36 — Gerund clauses

**Progression:** an _-ing_ clause in the subject slot, then an object inside that
clause, followed by the same clause form in an object slot. The cumulative band
takes the gerund out of the subject slot: one after a preposition, one with a
subject of its own.

**Plain**

1. Waiting quietly takes real patience.
2. Swimming daily builds steady strength.
3. Reading old maps requires practice.

**Varied**

4. Repairing the gate took an hour.
5. Carrying those boxes exhausted us.
6. She enjoys reading old maps.
7. The children avoided crossing the busy road.

**Cumulative**

8. Repairing the roadside gate took all morning.
9. She apologised for arriving late.
10. The children's shouting woke the baby. †

### Lesson 37 — Passive voice

**Progression:** a transitive passive with and without a named agent, then
two-object and object-complement passives. Earlier modifiers and clauses enter
only after the participant change is secure.

**Plain**

1. The old gate was securely locked.
2. The gate was locked by the caretaker.
3. Those important letters were delivered yesterday.

**Varied**

4. The damaged engine was repaired by a mechanic.
5. The guests were offered fresh water.
6. Young Mara was elected team captain.
7. The narrow road was blocked by fallen branches.

**Cumulative**

8. The riverside station was restored by local volunteers.
9. The driver was given a replacement vehicle.
10. The classroom was considered suitable after every window was repaired.

### Lesson 38 — Interjections and sentence-edge words

**Progression:** clear interjections, then adverb phrases functioning as
supplements. Later items carry fuller clause frames while the edge material
remains outside their slots.

**Plain**

1. Oh, the gate is open.
2. Well, we can wait outside.
3. Hey, that bus is leaving.

**Varied**

4. Fortunately, nobody nearby was badly hurt.
5. Surprisingly, the old engine started.
6. Honestly, I forgot the address.
7. Sadly, our youngest guest missed the train.

**Cumulative**

8. Fortunately, the careful mechanic found the loose cable.
9. Surprisingly, the bridge that the storm damaged remained open.
10. Sadly, our guide had given them the wrong time.

### Lesson 39 — Punctuation is evidence

**Progression:** begin with punctuation that supports familiar supplements and
appositives, then use commas to suggest clause boundaries and attachment. Items 8 and 9 are
the same six words, and the commas are the whole difference between all of them
and only some of them. The words and grammatical tests must still support every

**Plain**

1. Well, the heavy rain outside has finally stopped.
2. Mara, our patient team captain, waved.
3. The brass bell rang, and the heavy doors opened.

**Varied**

4. Before the last bus arrived, our guests gathered their bags.
5. When the gate opened, the visitors collected their bags.
6. The mechanic checked the brakes, but the driver heard another noise.
7. Surprisingly, the old bridge that crossed the river remained open.

**Cumulative**

8. The visitors who had missed their train waited.
9. The visitors, who had missed their train, waited. †

### Lesson 40 — Final synthesis

**Progression:** one embedded clause, then add modification, auxiliary structure,
voice, coordination, and nonfinite clauses. The final sentence must support two
natural attachment readings that the learner can defend.

**Plain**

1. The inspector calmly reported that the wiring had failed.
2. The inspector who arrived late reported that the wiring had failed.
3. Our guide explained why the narrow bridge remained closed.

**Varied**

4. The mechanic who checked the engine said that a cable had broken.
5. The driver was given a map before the last road was closed.
6. The rain stopped and the volunteers began repairing the damaged roof.
7. Reading the warning that our guide provided prevented a serious mistake.

**Cumulative**

8. Fortunately, the visitors who had missed their train found a room nearby.
9. The station was restored by volunteers, and the town opened a library.
10. The inspector discussed the report about the damaged bridge with the engineer.
