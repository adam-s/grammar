## Central generalization

An active clause and its matching passive can describe the same basic event and
the same participants while putting those participants in different grammatical
places. That difference gives writers options for what starts the clause, what
stays in focus, and whether the agent is named. The tree records the first
fact, not the reason for the choice.

For the matched pair below, under the ordinary one-event reading, the facts
about who dredged what are the same:

> _The contractors dredged the harbour._
>
> _The harbour was dredged by the contractors._

The first makes _the contractors_ the subject and _the harbour_ the direct
object. The second makes _the harbour_ the subject and puts _the contractors_
in a _by_-phrase. The contractors remain the agents and the harbour remains
what was dredged. The structural relationship changes; the event roles do not.

That is not a claim that every active/passive-looking paraphrase has identical
truth conditions. It is safe only when the verb, tense, aspect, modality,
quantity, negation, and named participants match. Leaving out the _by_-phrase
does not make a third sentence false, but it leaves the agent's identity
unasserted: _Those deeds were filed_ cannot mean exactly the same thing as
_The contractors filed those deeds_. An active paraphrase such as _Someone
filed those deeds_ supplies an overt indefinite agent; _They filed those deeds_
usually asks the reader to recover a particular group. Neither tree settles
whether either added wording fits the discourse.

## The grammatical contrast

| Clause                                                  | Structural relationship in this course                                                                                              | Event relationship                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _The contractors dredged the harbour._ (`c37-a`)        | _the contractors_ is `NP / subject`; _dredged_ is the active verb; _the harbour_ is `NP / direct object`.                           | The contractors are the agent; the harbour is the affected participant.             |
| _The harbour was dredged by the contractors._ (`c37-d`) | _the harbour_ is `NP / subject`; _was_ is passive auxiliary; _dredged_ is the passive verb; _by the contractors_ is a PP adverbial. | The harbour is still the affected participant; the contractors are still the agent. |
| _Those deeds were filed._ (`c37-b`)                     | _those deeds_ is the passive subject; the clause has passive _were_ plus _filed_, with no agent phrase.                             | The filing event is asserted, but no agent identity is supplied.                    |

The course calls the _by_-phrase an adverbial. Other grammatical descriptions
call it an optional agent phrase or an adjunct. The framework choice does not
change the lesson's point: in a passive, the active subject is no longer the
clause subject and may be omitted from the wording.

## What the contrast can show

The side-by-side `c37-a`/`c37-d` diagrams are the main evidence. They should
make the following relationship visible:

1. The noun phrase that was the active object is the passive subject.
2. A passive _by_-phrase can name the active agent without restoring it to the
   subject slot.
3. The same people, place, action, and time can be described through either
   structure.

The figure should then say what it cannot show. In a real passage, a writer may
choose the passive because _the harbour_ is already the topic, because the
harbour rather than the contractors is the useful starting point, because the
agent is unknown, obvious, irrelevant, or deliberately withheld, or for a
mixture of those reasons. English often puts given information early and newer
information later, but that is a tendency, not a sentence-level rule. A tree
does not mark what a reader already knows, what the writer treats as news, or
the writer's motive.

This is why communicative choice is not a new structural label. _The harbour
was dredged by the contractors_ has one passive structure whether it answers
“What happened to the harbour?”, keeps a known topic in place, or deliberately
foregrounds the contractors at the end. The different purposes do not create
different parses, and the app cannot fairly grade them.

## Diagnostics and their limits

| Evidence                                                                                      | What it supports                                                                                 | Where it stops                                                                                                                                                         |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Turn _The harbour was dredged by the contractors_ into _The contractors dredged the harbour_. | Lesson 37's passive analysis: a promoted object, a demoted agent, and passive verb form line up. | It identifies a structural relation; it does not prove why the writer chose that voice or that every paraphrase preserves every truth condition.                       |
| _Be_ plus a past participle.                                                                  | Evidence for a passive in an ordinary case such as _was dredged_.                                | It is not sufficient alone. _The gates were closed_ has both a passive-event reading and a state reading in the course.                                                |
| A _by_-phrase.                                                                                | It can identify the agent in a passive and make the matched event comparison precise.            | The phrase is optional. Its absence is not evidence that the clause is active, that no agent exists, or that the writer is concealing one.                             |
| “Does the same event make both clauses true?”                                                 | A useful check for the named-agent pair.                                                         | It relies on a supplied context. Different tense, aspect, negation, quantifiers, modality, an added agent, or a different intended reading can change the proposition. |
| First position and given/new order.                                                           | A reason a reader may infer from a surrounding passage.                                          | It is discourse evidence, not a test for voice and not a guaranteed order in every English sentence.                                                                   |

No diagnostic selects the better voice in isolation. That question needs a
passage, an audience, and a purpose, so it belongs in explanation rather than
practice scoring.

## Common summaries, and what they leave out

| Summary                                               | What it gets right                                                 | What it leaves out                                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “The passive turns the sentence around.”              | It points to the changed subject/object relationship.              | It risks making a construction sound like a word-order trick. Passive voice also changes verb form and demotes the active subject.             |
| “The passive puts the receiver first.”                | The affected participant often becomes subject.                    | “Receiver” is too narrow for every verb; the promoted participant may be a theme, goal, recipient, experiencer, or another role.               |
| “The passive hides the agent.”                        | An agentless passive does not state the agent's identity.          | A passive can name the agent in a _by_-phrase. Omission may be ordinary economy, not evasion, and an active intransitive can omit a cause too. |
| “Use active voice.”                                   | Active voice is often direct when the agent matters.               | It turns a contextual choice into a false rule and ignores topic, flow, focus, unknown agents, and cases where the passive is clearer.         |
| “The two versions mean the same thing.”               | The complete `c37-a`/`c37-d` pair can describe the same event.     | Their structure and information packaging differ; agentless or otherwise changed versions need not state the same proposition.                 |
| “Put old information first and new information last.” | It describes a real English preference that passive can help meet. | It is neither a definition of passive nor a universal ordering rule.                                                                           |

## Sources opened and read

- Catherine Anderson, _Essentials of Linguistics_, [§9.3, “Thematic Roles and
  Passive Sentences”](https://pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics/chapter/9-4-thematic-roles-and-passive-sentences/), read in full. It distinguishes grammatical subject from agent, shows the same participant roles with different active/passive grammatical roles, and treats the passive as a morphosyntactic reversal of the usual mapping.
- Nigel A. Caplan, University of Delaware, [“Given-New Information
  Structure”](https://udel.pressbooks.pub/language/chapter/given-new-information-structure/), read in full. It describes the tendency to put given material early and new material later, identifies passive voice as one resource for that pattern, and explicitly says the tendency is not a strict rule.
- UK Department for Education, [_English
  glossary_](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/244216/English_Glossary.pdf), entries for _passive_, _object_, _participle_, and _subject_ read. It supports the course-level analysis: the active object becomes the passive subject, the active subject is optionally expressed in a _by_-phrase, and passive form requires more than a passive-like meaning.
- Catherine Anderson, Bronwyn Bjorkman, Derek Denis, Julianne Doner, Margaret Grant, Nathan Sanders, and Ai Taniguchi, _Essentials of Linguistics_, 2nd ed., [§6.22 practice material on passive counterparts](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics2/chapter/practice-time-3/), read in full. Its passive exercise notes that constructing active counterparts often requires inventing an agent. That supports a narrower claim about an agentless passive's active paraphrases, not the false claim that it has no related active counterpart.

## Rejected

- **“The passive is an evasive form.”** A writer can omit an agent to evade
  responsibility, but a structural label cannot establish that motive. A
  passive can also name its agent, and agentless active clauses exist.
- **“Both trees state exactly the same facts.”** The controlled full pair can,
  but the trees do not encode discourse packaging and an omitted or supplied
  agent changes what the sentence explicitly identifies.
- **A voice-choice quiz.** No syntax-only test can choose the better voice
  without a context. Giving credit for that choice would make a contextual
  judgment look like a structural fact.
- **A new label for topic, givenness, focus, or communicative purpose.** These
  help explain a choice, but they do not divide the course's passive trees into
  new grammatical types.
