# Lesson 5 — Find the head

Researched 28 August 2026. An author's dossier, not a page for a learner. See
[../01-introduction/README.md](../01-introduction/README.md) for why.

**Status:** This dossier measures the built corpus. [sentences.md](sentences.md) proposes replacements that are not yet parsed or accepted as course data.

## What the lesson decides

| Decision   | In plain words             |
| ---------- | -------------------------- |
| `form:N`   | this word is a noun        |
| `form:Nom` | the layer the head sits in |

The head function itself arrived at lesson 3 on the verb, so what is new here is
applying it to a noun phrase.

**`form:Nom` moved here from lesson 16 on 28 August, and the reason only showed
up when the sentences were built.** A noun's head sits _inside_ a `Nom` whenever
the phrase has a premodifier or a postmodifier. With `Nom` arriving at 16, every
one of those pruned away at lesson 5 — head and all — so _The key to the cabinet
vanished_ asked the learner for a subject, a predicate and a verb, and **no noun
head at all**. Seven picks, against nine for _The clock stopped_.

So the only noun phrase whose head lesson 5 could ask about was one with nothing
else in it, which is exactly a noun phrase with no competitor. **The lesson could
not teach its own subject**, and neither this dossier nor the sentence proposal
noticed, because both reasoned about sentences rather than about targets.

## The finding

**Every sentence in this lesson has exactly one noun.**

_The river froze. The fire spread. The plane landed. The kettle whistled._ Three
words each, one noun each, and that noun is the head because there is nothing
else it could be.

A lesson called _Find the head_ in which the head is the only candidate is not a
lesson, it is a naming exercise. The learner cannot get it wrong and so cannot
learn the test.

**The sentences this lesson needs are sitting in lesson 4.** _The man in the
coat laughed_ has two nouns and a head that is not the nearer one. Lesson 4
teaches no label and holds the material; lesson 5 teaches the label and holds
none. The two lessons have each other's sentences.

## The tests

### Omission

Take the candidate away and see what survives. _The man in the coat laughed_ →
_The man laughed_ is a sentence; _The in the coat laughed_ is not. The word you
cannot remove is the head.

### Agreement

This is the strongest test, because the head controls the verb and nothing else
in the phrase does.

> A verb must agree with its subject, not with the object of a prepositional
> phrase, which often comes between the subject and the verb.

_The book on the shelves **is** heavy_, not _are_. The head is _book_, however
many shelves there are. Teaching sources reach for the same trick this lesson
needs:

> "it may help to envision the sentence without the prepositional phrase. So
> 'The book on the shelves is…' becomes simply 'The book is…'"

That is the omission test, arrived at from the other direction.

### Substitution

The pro-form replaces the whole phrase, and what it leaves behind tells you what
was inside. Covered at lesson 4 and used again at lesson 7.

## Other names for this

| This app                     | Elsewhere                                      |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| the `N` with function `head` | simple subject, when the phrase is the subject |
| the `NP` around it           | complete subject                               |

The pairing is worth stating plainly to an author: **lesson 5 is where "simple
subject" finally becomes a separate thing from "complete subject."** A learner
who met those terms at school has been holding one idea since lesson 1 and can
now split it.

## Shortcut register

| Shortcut                                  | What defeats it                                 | In the course?            |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| The head is the only noun                 | any subject with two nouns                      | **no** — all ten have one |
| The head is the last noun before the verb | _the man in the coat_, _the key to the cabinet_ | **no**                    |
| The head is the second word               | any longer noun phrase                          | **no**                    |

Three of three. There is no sentence in this lesson where naming the head
requires a test.

[sentences.md](sentences.md) fixes this: it has _A box of tools fell_ and _The
key to the cabinet vanished_, both of which put the head first and a decoy last,
and five of its ten put the head somewhere other than last. Neither sentence is
in the built corpus.

## What this should change

1. **Lesson 5 needs subjects with more than one noun.** This is the single
   clearest defect in Stage 1 and the fix costs nothing, because lesson 4
   already contains ten suitable sentences.
2. **The agreement test deserves a hint.** It is the one test that survives into
   real writing, where proximity agreement is a common error, and the app does
   not currently mention it anywhere.
3. **Lesson 5 is the same length as lessons 1 and 3.** Three words, again.

## Rejected

- **"A noun is a person, place or thing."** It picks out most nouns and it is
  not a test. Worse here than elsewhere, because this lesson's real work is
  choosing between two nouns, and a definition that calls both of them nouns
  settles nothing.
- **Teaching the head through examples with one noun.** That is what the lesson
  currently does.
