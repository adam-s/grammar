## Central generalization

A **gerund-participial clause** in this course is a nonfinite _-ing_ clause
whose whole structure fills a noun-phrase-like slot in a larger structure. The
verb inside still takes the dependents a verb takes; the outer clause decides
whether the whole _-ing_ clause is a subject, direct object, or complement of a
preposition.

The central distinction is between internal form and external function. In
_She enjoys [reading maps]_, _maps_ is the direct object of _reading_, while
_reading maps_ is the direct object of _enjoys_. Calling the whole clause
"noun-like" does not turn _reading_ into a noun or make the clause an NP.

## Form, function, and the name

Traditional school grammar commonly calls the noun-slot use a **gerund** and
uses **present participle** for an _-ing_ form in a progressive or a noun
modifier. That is a useful first contrast, but it hides that the forms are
identical and that their labels have changed across grammars. Huddleston and
Pullum use **gerund-participle** for the shared verbal _-ing_ form and
distinguish the clauses by their functions and distributions instead. The
course's **gerund-participial clause** is a defensible teaching name for the
noun-slot cases, but it is a course choice, not the only analysis of English.

| Question                                  | _She enjoys reading maps._                                         | _The clerk was auditing the ledger._                                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What is the _-ing_ word doing internally? | _reading_ heads a predicate and takes _maps_ as its direct object. | _auditing_ is the lexical verb in the progressive verb phrase and takes _the ledger_ as its direct object. |
| What does the full run do outside itself? | _reading maps_ is a direct-object clause after _enjoys_.           | _was auditing the ledger_ is the matrix predicate.                                                         |
| Course analysis                           | `Cl`, `kind:nominal`, `fin:gerund-participial`                     | progressive auxiliary plus verb; no embedded _-ing_ clause                                                 |

Lesson 35 already makes the other live _-ing_ contrast available: _The child
standing by the gate waved_ has a noun-modifying clause, stored as
`kind:relative` and `fin:participial`. The course gives both that present
participial modifier and the noun-slot clause a separate label even though
reference grammars may treat their _-ing_ verb form as one gerund-participle
form. Learner copy should teach the visible difference in external job, not
claim that the suffix itself contains two reliably different forms.

## Subjects: absent, understood, and overt

None of the nine target clauses has an overt subject. In the subject examples,
the event is generic or supplied by context. In _The children avoided crossing
the road_, _We finished after packing every lamp_, and _She apologised for
arriving late_, readers normally understand the matrix subject as the crosser,
packer, or arrival. That understood participant is not a word inside the lower
clause, and the course model records no controller relation. It must not draw or
grade one merely because the likely interpretation is clear.

English can pronounce a subject in some _-ing_ clauses: _We objected to Kim
joining the party_, _Do you mind me asking?_, and, in a more formal style, _We
objected to Kim's joining the party_. Reference accounts distinguish the plain
or accusative form from the genitive form; genitives are optional in the
relevant complement constructions, vary with style and NP type, and are not
available in every _-ing_ construction. Possessive-looking forms are therefore
not a definition or a dependable learner test for a gerund-participial clause.

The planned _The children's shouting woke the baby_ is rightly outside the
current lesson. The app has no representation for the possessive NP relation,
no approved fixture for an overt subject inside a gerund-participial clause,
and no way to represent the relation between an unspoken lower subject and a
matrix NP. This is a model limit, not evidence that English lacks the pattern.

## Nouns ending in _-ing_ are a separate contrast

An _-ing_ spelling can also be a noun. Compare _He was expelled for [killing
the birds]_ with _She witnessed [the killing of the birds]_. The first lower
head behaves as a verb; the second is an NP headed by a gerundial noun. This
contrast matters because "a gerund is a verbal noun" blurs the very distinction
lesson 36 needs.

Use several converging properties, not one magic test:

| Evidence              | Verb-headed gerund-participial clause                         | NP headed by a gerundial noun                        | Limit                                                                                                               |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Complement            | _killing the birds_ takes an NP direct object.                | _the killing of the birds_ uses an _of_-PP.          | Some verbs themselves take PP complements, so _of_ is not a standalone test.                                        |
| Modifier              | _wantonly killing the birds_ uses an adverb.                  | _the wanton killing of the birds_ uses an adjective. | Many short examples supply no modifier at all.                                                                      |
| Determiner and number | _the killing the birds_ is not the relevant verbal structure. | _the killing of the birds_; often _these killings_.  | Not every noun is countable or plural in the intended sense.                                                        |
| Outer substitution    | _She objected to it_ can stand for the whole clause.          | _She witnessed it_ can stand for the NP.             | Successful _it_ substitution supports constituency and an NP-like outer slot; it does not tell a clause from an NP. |

Bare _writing_ can genuinely leave the analysis open: _Kim had been talking
about writing_ can mean writing as an activity or a noun-like subject. The
current practice set avoids that ambiguity because every target has a verbal
object or adverbial, but it also supplies no noun contrast. A revision can use
the controlled _killing_ pair as ungraded explanatory prose only if its parse
is approved; it should not call a bare _-ing_ word a noun or gerund solely from
its position.

## Diagnostics and their limits

| Procedure                                                                                                   | Evidence it gives                                                                                    | Limit                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Bracket the lower predicate and its dependents: _[reading maps]_, _[arriving late]_.                        | Shows that the lower verb has its own object or adverbial.                                           | An _-ing_ form in a progressive also has dependents, so this does not decide the outer job.                                                                                           |
| Find the complete run's outer slot: subject, direct object, or complement after a preposition.              | Distinguishes the course's noun-slot target from a progressive VP or lesson 35's noun modifier.      | The course's `kind:nominal` is a label for this external relation; it does not make the clause an NP or settle every framework's terminology.                                         |
| Where meaning permits, replace the full run with _it_: _She enjoys it_; _She apologised for it_.            | Supports the clause's constituency and noun-phrase-like distribution in these examples.              | Pronoun choice depends on meaning and context. The test does not apply equally naturally to every subject clause and cannot distinguish a clause from an NP.                          |
| Change the matrix time: _She enjoys reading maps_ / _She enjoyed reading maps_.                             | _enjoys/enjoyed_ changes while _reading_ does not carry primary tense or agreement.                  | The same is true of other nonfinite forms; it does not distinguish gerund-participial from infinitival or participial clauses.                                                        |
| Compare the controlled pair: _Auditing the ledger revealed an error_ / _The clerk was auditing the ledger_. | The same lower verb and object occur in a noun-slot clause once and in a progressive predicate once. | The surrounding words make the contrast unusually clear. Do not reduce the test to "look for _was_"; progressive auxiliaries vary in form and a bare _-ing_ word can have other uses. |

## Teaching summaries to keep or qualify

| Common summary                       | What it gets right                                                                 | What learner copy must add or change                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "A gerund is a verb used as a noun." | It points to the noun-slot behavior of the whole _-ing_ clause.                    | Say that the **clause** has the outer job while its head remains a verb; distinguish genuine _-ing_ nouns.                   |
| "A gerund ends in _-ing_."           | The current target does use the _-ing_ form.                                       | The ending alone also appears in progressives and noun modifiers; it cannot classify the structure.                          |
| "Gerunds are subjects."              | Five live targets are subject clauses.                                             | The live set also has two direct objects and two preposition complements.                                                    |
| "Use a possessive before a gerund."  | A genitive subject is possible in some formal complement clauses.                  | Plain or accusative subjects are also standard, conditions vary, and the model cannot draw the relevant possessive relation. |
| "Replace it with a noun."            | Replacement is useful evidence for these direct-object and PP-complement examples. | It supports an external slot; it is not a definition and cannot distinguish clauses from NPs.                                |

## Revision direction

1. Keep the opening claim, but qualify it: an _-ing_ clause can have a
   noun-phrase-like **external job** while its internal head remains a verb.
   Do not call the clause a noun phrase or call the test a definition.
2. Make `fix-gerund-after-preposition` the main visual only for the claim it
   proves: the clause completes _for_, while the larger PP modifies
   _apologised_. Add a subject or direct-object fixture before claiming that a
   single diagram shows all three jobs.
3. Put _Auditing the ledger revealed an error_ beside _The clerk was auditing
   the ledger_ in the learner page. Explain the changing outer job, not a
   supposed difference hidden in the suffix.
4. Give the bracket-and-outer-slot procedure before the _it_ replacement. State
   beside it that replacement is evidence for this construction, not proof that
   the structure is an NP.
5. Link backward to lesson 35's _standing_ modifier as the other course use of
   an _-ing_ form. Link forward to lesson 37 only for the separate question of
   what a following _be_ auxiliary means; passive _be_ takes a past participle,
   while progressive _be_ takes _-ing_.
6. Do not add an overt-subject or possessive exercise until the model can show
   both the lower subject and any required possessive relation. Do not label an
   unrepresented controller in feedback.
7. Add a reviewed noun-versus-clause fixture only if the page needs that
   misconception. _Killing the birds_ / _the killing of the birds_ is the
   cleanest contrast; a bare _writing_ example is not.

## Sources opened and read

- Geoffrey K. Pullum, [“Non-Finite Clauses”](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/grammar/nonfiniteclauses.html), University of Edinburgh. Read in full. It gives the nonfinite-clause inventory, treats _writing a novel_ as gerund-participial, and notes that such clauses may lack an overt subject. It also explains why a missing complement-clause subject needs an interpretation without making every case a simple same-subject rule.
- Richard Donaldson, [“Some Family Relations: A Study of Control in English”](https://www.lel.ed.ac.uk/~gpullum/Donaldson2021.pdf), University of Edinburgh, 2021, pp. 93–96 and 243–46. Read the cited sections. This scholarly account records the remaining genitive/plain-subject distinction, gives _them/their annually gathering the corn_, and distinguishes historically separate gerund and participle traditions from their modern formal convergence. It supports the dossier's caution on genitives and understood subjects.
- Michael Swan, [“Language terminology”](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/about/practical-english-usage/language-terminology.html), _Practical English Usage_, Oxford University Press. Read in full. Its pedagogical definitions of _gerund_, _present participle_, _progressive_, _noun phrase_, and _complement_ show the common classroom terminology that the page must translate without treating it as the only analysis.
- Huddleston and Pullum, [_The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language_](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-grammar-of-the-english-language/94B7D7DE80C7D8D51A14E5EE353B3DE5), 2002, Chapter 3, pp. 80–83, and Chapter 14, pp. 1187–1265. The terminology and diagnostics reported here are the course's reference framework: gerund-participle versus gerundial noun, direct-object versus _of_-PP complementation, adverb versus adjective modification, determiner and plural evidence, and the qualified distribution of genitive subjects. The Cambridge chapter preview was available but its full text requires access; the two open University of Edinburgh sources above were read for the relevant account and examples.

## Rejected as lesson content

- **A suffix-only definition.** It cannot distinguish the current target from
  the progressive or lesson 35's modifier.
- **“Gerund = verbal noun.”** It blurs a verb-headed clause with a genuine
  gerundial noun and hides the lower verb's object or adverbial.
- **A rule requiring possessive subjects.** It is stylistically and
  structurally too narrow, and the course cannot model the relation honestly.
- **Control terminology as a scoring target.** The interpretation is real, but
  the model stores neither the unspoken subject nor its link to a controller.
