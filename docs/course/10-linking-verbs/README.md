## Central generalization

A **linking verb** licenses a subject complement: a phrase that predicates a
property or identity of the subject. In _The soup tasted salty_, _salty_ does
not name something the soup tasted. It says what the soup was like in respect of
its taste.

The verb still contributes meaning. _Tasted_ reports flavour, while _was_ only
states a property. An equals sign or a _be_ paraphrase can make the
subject–complement relationship easier to see, but it cannot define every
linking use or preserve the full meaning of the original sentence.

This course calls the non-_be_ verbs in this frame `Vlink` and gives _be_ its
own type, `Vbe`, in lesson 11. That is a course decision, not a claim that all
reference grammars draw the category boundary in exactly that place. Reference
grammars often call the larger construction **copular** or
**complex-intransitive**.

## The structure the lesson teaches

| Decision                 | What it says about _The soup tasted salty_                 |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| `form:Adj`               | _salty_ is an adjective.                                   |
| `form:AdjP`              | The adjective is the head of the adjective phrase _salty_. |
| `func:subjectComplement` | That adjective phrase predicates a property of _the soup_. |
| `vt:Vlink`               | _tasted_ licenses this subject-complement frame.           |

The form and function are separate claims. An adjective phrase can be a subject
complement here, but adjective phrases can do other work elsewhere. The
function comes from its relationship to the verb and subject, not from the word
class alone.

The complement is a dependent licensed by the verb. That is the structural
reason it differs from an optional detail. The course's `Vlink` examples all
use adjective phrases. Lesson 11 begins the noun-phrase case. Some grammars also
call prepositional and adverb phrases after copular verbs subject complements;
this course treats the _be_ location pattern separately as a required adverbial
in lesson 14.

## What the live evidence shows

The page's fixed fixture, _The soup tasted salty_, cleanly displays the first
four decisions. It does not, by itself, show that the same word can appear in
another frame. The practice set supplies that missing contrast:

| Same verb | Linking frame           | Other live frame                                        | What changes                                                                                   |
| --------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _grow_    | _The sky grew dark._    | _A farmer grew some potatoes._ / _Those children grew._ | _dark_ predicates a property of the sky; _potatoes_ is an object; the third use takes neither. |
| _feel_    | _The water felt cold._  | _She felt the rough cloth._                             | _cold_ predicates a property of the water; _the rough cloth_ names a separate thing.           |
| _turn_    | _Our milk turned sour._ | _The tide turned._ (lesson 8)                           | _sour_ is a subject complement; the earlier use has no complement.                             |

Verb type belongs to a verb **in a particular frame**, not permanently to a
spelling. These contrasts correct two weak claims in the earlier dossier: the
live set no longer contains only linking frames, and it no longer requires a
learner to identify a verb type from the lesson title alone.

The practice set has a deliberate limit: all seven linking examples are `NP V
AdjP`. That makes the adjective phrase available on the day it is taught, but
the learner does not yet meet noun-phrase, prepositional-phrase, or
adverb-phrase subject complements. The page should say that it explains one
frequent shape, not define the category by the presence of an adjective.

## Diagnostics, with their limits

No single classroom test identifies every linking construction. The page should
give converging evidence rather than promote one paraphrase into a definition.

| Evidence                                                                                                              | What it earns in this lesson                                                                                                                                                           | Where it stops                                                                                                                                                     |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Subject relation:** Can the phrase after the verb be understood as a property of the subject? _The soup is salty._  | It distinguishes _salty_ from an object: the soup is the thing described.                                                                                                              | It is a meaning-based paraphrase. _Tasted_ adds a flavour meaning that _was_ removes, and a plausible _be_ sentence is not a grammatical test by itself.           |
| **Phrase form:** Is the following phrase an adjective phrase?                                                         | In this course, an adjective phrase directly completing _taste_, _seem_, _grow_, or _feel_ is strong evidence for a subject complement. A direct object cannot be an adjective phrase. | An adjective phrase can also be an optional secondary predicate: _The hikers came home tired_. The verb's frame still has to license the phrase as its complement. |
| **Frame contrast:** Does the same verb take an object, no complement, or a subject complement in different sentences? | _Grow_ and _feel_ show that the construction, not the word alone, determines the answer.                                                                                               | A learner needs an actual contrast or a known verb frame; this is not a test that can be applied mechanically to every new verb.                                   |
| **Passive:** Can a putative object become the subject of a passive?                                                   | A real direct object usually can; a subject complement cannot.                                                                                                                         | This is strong structural evidence but arrives only with passive voice in lesson 37, so it is not a lesson 10 procedure.                                           |

The earlier dossier called substitution with _be_ “reliable.” That overclaims.
It is useful here as a controlled paraphrase: _The soup tasted salty_ and _The
soup was salty_ share a subject-property relation. It should be presented that
way.

## How sources and teaching materials summarise this topic

| Common summary                                       | What it gets right                                                     | What it leaves out                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “A linking verb links the subject to a description.” | It directs attention back to the subject rather than toward an object. | “Links” can stay vague unless the page names the predication: the complement says something about the subject.                                                                      |
| “A linking verb is an equals sign.”                  | _The soup was salty_ makes the one-participant relation visible.       | _Tasted_, _seemed_, and _became_ add meaning, so they are not simply _be_. The comparison is a paraphrase, not an analysis.                                                         |
| “Use an adjective after a linking verb.”             | The course's live `Vlink` examples all take adjective phrases.         | Subject complements have more than one phrase form, and _well_ can be an adjective meaning healthy. _I feel bad_ and _I feel badly_ are both grammatical but mean different things. |
| “Memorise the linking verbs.”                        | Some verbs frequently appear in this construction.                     | _feel_, _grow_, _turn_, _look_, _smell_, and _taste_ can appear in other frames. A list cannot decide the use in a particular sentence.                                             |

The best learner-facing summary is relational: a linking use lets the verb's
complement say something about the subject. The examples must then show why that
phrase is not an object and why the same word can work differently in another
frame.

## Shortcut audit

| Shortcut                                                        | What defeats it                                                                                | In the live set?                                                                           |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| A verb has one permanent type.                                  | _grow_ has linking, transitive, and intransitive uses; _feel_ has linking and transitive uses. | **Yes** — five sentences establish the contrasts.                                          |
| The first phrase after a verb is always an object.              | _The soup tasted salty_: _salty_ describes the soup and is an adjective phrase, not an object. | **Yes** — this is the lesson's central decision.                                           |
| A linking verb is a word from a memorised list.                 | The same listed word can take a different frame.                                               | **Yes**, if the page foregrounds the _grow_ or _feel_ contrast.                            |
| A word after a linking verb must be an adjective.               | _He is a doctor_ introduces a noun-phrase subject complement in lesson 11.                     | **Not yet** — keep the claim qualified on this page and make lesson 11 carry the contrast. |
| _Well_ is always an adverb, so it cannot follow a linking verb. | _I feel well_ uses _well_ as an adjective meaning healthy.                                     | **Not in the current set** — do not teach “never _smells well_” as an error.               |

## Revision direction

1. **Open with predication, not connection alone.** Say that the complement
   describes or identifies the subject; then name the relationship as a subject
   complement. Do not say that the phrase merely “points back.”
2. **Retitle the _be_ section as a paraphrase.** It should ask the learner to
   make the relation visible with _be_, then state that the original verb adds
   its own meaning. Remove any claim that this substitution proves the verb is
   linking.
3. **Use the live _grow_ contrast as the main complication.** A figure or
   contrast based on the three live _grow_ frames would show the course's main
   generalization more directly than a final rule block. Add approved fixtures
   if the existing `fix-vlink` and `fix-vtr` cannot render that contrast.
4. **Keep the adjective phrase example, but state its scope.** The page may
   teach adjective phrases as the first form of subject complement without
   implying that all complements are adjectives. Lesson 11 must make the
   noun-phrase contrast explicit.
5. **Remove the false good/well correction.** If the page discusses adjective
   versus adverb forms, use a meaning contrast such as _I feel bad_ / _I feel
   badly_, and say what changes. Do not mark one as ungrammatical.

## Sources actually opened and read

- Rodney Huddleston, [_A Short Overview of English
  Syntax_](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/grammar/overview.html), §§5.2–5.6.
  This authorial overview of the _Cambridge Grammar of the English Language_
  defines complements as verb-licensed dependents, separates objects from
  predicative complements, and treats the construction as belonging to verbs in
  their clause frames, not as a permanent label on every verb spelling.
- Cambridge English Grammar Today, [_Adjective phrases:
  functions_](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/nl/grammatica/british-grammar/adjective-phrases-functions).
  The page identifies adjective phrases after _be, seem, become, feel, smell,_
  and _taste_ as predicative complements and gives the same
  subject–verb–adjective pattern used in the lesson.
- Nigel A. Caplan, University of Delaware, [_Talking About Language: Linking
  Verbs_](https://udel.pressbooks.pub/language/chapter/linking-verbs/). This
  open textbook records the common “relating verb” explanation, distinguishes a
  linked description from a direct object, and illustrates the range of phrase
  forms used as complements. Its functional terminology is not adopted by the
  course.
- American College of the Mediterranean, [_Glossary of Rhetoric, Grammar, and
  Usage_](https://acd.pressbooks.pub/rediscoveringliteraturethroughwriting/chapter/glossary-of-rhetoric-grammar-and-usage/).
  Read for the _feel bad_ / _feel badly_ distinction: both are grammatical but
  differ in meaning. This blocks the prior proposed “not _smells well_” rule.

## Rejected or qualified

- **“A linking verb is not an action verb.”** Rejected. It erases the meaning
  contributed by verbs such as _become_, _seem_, and _taste_, and it gives no
  structural reason for the complement.
- **“Replace the verb with _be_ to confirm it is linking.”** Qualified. The
  paraphrase is useful evidence of a subject-property relation in a controlled
  example; it does not independently classify every verb use.
- **“A linking verb takes an adjective, not an adverb.”** Qualified. It is true
  of the adjective-complement frame taught here, but it must not become a
  universal spelling rule about words such as _well_ or a definition of subject
  complements.
- **“Linking verbs are a list.”** Rejected. The unit of analysis is the verb in
  a sentence frame; the live _grow_ and _feel_ contrasts are the evidence.
