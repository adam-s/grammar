## Central generalization

**Form** names the kind of unit and the structure that makes it that kind of
unit. **Function** names the relationship that unit has to a larger phrase or
clause. The two answers describe the same words at different levels; neither
answer predicts the other.

A prepositional phrase is a PP because it has a preposition and its complement.
That PP can be an adverbial in a clause, a complement inside another phrase, or
a modifier inside a noun phrase. Conversely, an adverbial can be an AdvP or a
PP, and later in the course it can be a noun phrase or a clause. The lesson
must establish both directions. “A PP can be required or optional” is one
useful consequence of the distinction, not its definition.

This is the course's analysis, not a claim that every grammar uses its labels
in the same way. The app calls a verb-selected place PP a **required
adverbial**; many modern grammars call it a locative complement. Both accounts
still separate the PP's form from its clause-level relationship.

## The relationships to show

| Unit and form         | Relationship in the larger structure            | Course evidence                                                                                        |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| _the children_ — NP   | subject in _The children devoured those books_  | A noun phrase can fill the subject slot.                                                               |
| _those books_ — NP    | direct object in the same clause                | The same form can fill a different clause slot.                                                        |
| _of the wood_ — PP    | complement of _out_ in _out of the wood_        | A PP can be inside a larger PP, not only beside a verb. `fix-prep-in-prep` shows this reading.         |
| _in the tunnel_ — PP  | adverbial in _The workers waited in the tunnel_ | The PP adds the setting of an otherwise complete event. `fix-subject-phrase-moved` shows this reading. |
| _very quietly_ — AdvP | adverbial in _She waited very quietly_          | `fix-adverb-phrase` shows a different form filling the same broad clause function.                     |

The course also has a later PP postmodifier: _the key to the door of the shed_
in c21-c. That is a useful forward connection, but `postmodifier` is lesson
21's label. Lesson 20 can describe the relationship in plain language without
asking the learner to use the later label.

The difference between word form and phrase form needs equal care. In _late_,
the word is an adverb and the one-word unit is an AdvP; the whole AdvP is the
adverbial. Do not present word class, phrase form, and clause function as three
rival names for one flat thing.

## What the current contrast actually establishes

The ten live practice sentences form five deliberate pairs. Each pair holds a
final PP constant in form and wording:

| Optional adverbial                            | Required adverbial in this course's analysis         |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| _The quartet rehearsed **in the hall**._      | _They put the piano **in the hall**._                |
| _The cat slept **on the sideboard**._         | _Another porter laid the tray **on the sideboard**._ |
| _The draught whistled **under the counter**._ | _The clerk placed the deeds **under the counter**._  |
| _The nurse waited **beside the bed**._        | _She placed the lamp **beside the bed**._            |
| _That rope swung **across the gap**._         | _The carpenter set the beam **across the gap**._     |

This is good, controlled evidence that a PP's words and final position do not
decide whether the verb selects a location. The _rehearsed/put_ and
_waited/placed_ pairs are especially clear.

It does **not** prove the central generalization as presently described. In the
data model, every one of these PPs has the function `adverbial`; the required
ones merely also carry `obligatory: true`. The contrast therefore shows two
statuses of a clause adverbial, not one PP changing from adverbial to
complement, subject, or modifier. The current static-page contrast repeats the
same limitation: `fix-vbe` and `fix-subject-phrase-moved` are both PPs with the
function `adverbial`, while also changing subject, verb, preposition, and noun.

The _laid_ and _set_ pairs are weaker than the other three. In a supportive
context, _laid the tray_ and _set the beam_ can recover a destination or allow
a related transitive sense. They should not carry a claim that removal always
produces an impossible sentence. Use _put_ or _place_ when the page needs a
clear first demonstration of a selected destination.

## Evidence and its limits

### First find the unit, then its relationship

Use the complement and boundary tests from lesson 19 to identify a PP: in _out
of the wood_, _of the wood_ is what _out_ takes, and the larger run can move
together. Those tests give evidence about form and constituent boundaries.
They do not, by themselves, tell the PP's function.

Then ask what contains the PP and what relationship it has there. A PP inside a
noun phrase can narrow which noun is meant; a PP beside a verb can set the
event, complete the verb's selected pattern, or be part of some other phrase.
Agreement, paraphrase, and a complete diagram can provide converging evidence.
No question based only on the preposition, the PP's meaning, or its position
settles the function.

### Removal tests selection, not form or every function

Hold the verb sense and context still, remove the **whole** PP, and read what
remains as an ordinary standalone clause.

- _The quartet rehearsed in the hall_ → _The quartet rehearsed._ The event is
  complete; the PP is optional.
- _They put the piano in the hall_ → _\*They put the piano._ In the ordinary
  caused-placement sense, the destination is missing; the PP is required in
  this course's analysis.

This is useful evidence about the verb's pattern. It does not define a PP, an
adverbial, or a complement. Context can license ellipsis; omitting the phrase
can change the verb's sense; and a sentence can be complete while less useful
or less informative. The learner should decide whether the original frame
survives, not whether the remaining words could ever make sense in conversation.

## Common summaries and what they omit

| Summary                                                | Useful part                                              | Missing limit                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “Form is what it is; function is what it does.”        | It separates two questions.                              | “Does” must mean a structural relationship to a larger unit, not a vague semantic job. It also needs to say which structural level each label describes.   |
| “Prepositional phrases work as adjectives or adverbs.” | It alerts learners that a PP can have more than one use. | It turns functions into word-class names, overlooks PP complements and subject complements, and does not explain the attachment that makes the difference. |
| “A PP after a verb is an adverbial.”                   | It often identifies an event-setting PP.                 | A PP can be selected by the verb, and a phrase after a noun can attach inside the noun phrase instead. Position alone is not a diagnostic.                 |
| “Required adverbials cannot be removed.”               | It gives a workable first test for a selected slot.      | It does not show that form and function are different, and it must preserve verb sense and context.                                                        |
| “The same form can do different jobs.”                 | It states one half of the generalization.                | It leaves out the reverse: several forms can realize the same function. It also invites learners to memorize labels rather than trace relationships.       |

## Current corpus: shortcuts and gaps

The corpus defeats “the words _in the hall_ always do the same thing” only when
the learner compares matched items. Its sequence introduces a more powerful
shortcut:

| Shortcut                                                                              | Why it can work now                                                   | What the corpus needs                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The first five are optional; the last five are required.                              | The ordering exactly tracks the answer.                               | Interleave both readings after the initial worked pair.                                                                                                         |
| An intransitive verb means optional; a transitive verb plus an object means required. | c20-a–e are `SV + PP` with `Vint`; c20-f–j are `SVO + PP` with `Vtr`. | Include both functions with more than one verb-frame shape, or make the task explicitly about the verb's selected relationship rather than the verb-type label. |
| A final PP is the lesson's whole answer.                                              | Every practice target is a final PP.                                  | Add a PP that is a complement within a PP and, when lesson-21 scope permits, one that belongs inside a noun phrase.                                             |
| Form tells whether a phrase is required.                                              | Nothing in the set has a non-PP adverbial.                            | Add a matched adverbial-function contrast with an AdvP and a PP.                                                                                                |

The practice set has a narrower, still valuable job: it rehearses the
required/optional distinction from lesson 14. It does not yet give the learner
an exercise that requires the full many-to-many form/function generalization.

## Revision implications

1. Replace the page's opening claim with the central generalization above. Do
   not say that a PP's function is simply “required” or “optional”; in the
   current model, both paired PPs are adverbials.
2. Use a main contrast that actually changes function while holding PP form
   visible: `fix-prep-in-prep` can show _of the wood_ as a PP complement, and
   `fix-subject-phrase-moved` can show _in the tunnel_ as a PP adverbial.
   Explain that the first belongs to the preposition and the second to the
   clause. Do not call these examples matched: they demonstrate two functions,
   not a controlled lexical contrast.
3. Add an approved, purpose-built static fixture with the same broad
   adverbial function realized by an AdvP and a PP—for example, two ordinary
   _wait_ clauses differing only in _very quietly_ and _in the tunnel_. The
   diagram must label each unit's form and the shared adverbial relationship.
   It is the missing reverse direction, so an unlabelled prose list is not
   enough.
4. Keep one _put/place_ versus _wait/rehearse_ pair as a separate, labelled
   example of verb selection. State its narrow conclusion beside the removal
   procedure and point back to lesson 14. The page should not reuse `fix-vbe`
   versus `fix-subject-phrase-moved` as its main proof: that contrast changes
   too much and duplicates lesson 14.
5. Rebuild the ten practice items around at least two decisions: PP form plus
   its relation to a host, and adverbial function plus its form. Interleave
   optional and required adverbials. Keep postmodifier labelling out of graded
   work until lesson 21, but a plain-language preview can support the forward
   connection.
6. Make the closing claim relational: a label becomes useful only when it
   answers one level of structure. The lesson should not close with a list of
   labels or a removal-test slogan.

Lesson 19 supplies PP form and internal complements. Lesson 21 supplies the
next visible case in which a PP belongs inside a noun phrase. The later clause
lessons should reuse this many-to-many distinction: clauses share a form while
filling different functions, and a function can be realized by more than one
form.

## Sources opened and read

- Bas Aarts and Ian Cushing, [“Making Grammar Meaningful”](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10107837/1/Aarts_Making%20grammar%20meaningful%20Cushing%20and%20Aarts%202019.pdf), _NATE Teaching English_ 19. The authors define form as category labels and function as roles in a sentence; they explicitly show the same NP as subject and object, and adverbials realized by adverbs, AdvPs, PPs, subordinate clauses, and NPs.
- Cambridge University Press, [“Subject complements,” _English Grammar Today_](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/parts-of-speech). It lists adjective, noun, adverb, and prepositional phrases as possible subject complements and distinguishes complements from optional adjuncts. Its terminology is evidence of the framework difference around locative PPs.
- British Council LearnEnglish, [“Noun phrases”](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/noun-phrases). Its worked discussion of _He puts the keys in the drawer_ confirms the PP's form while leaving its larger attachment as a separate question, and its noun-phrase discussion supplies the later postmodifier connection.

## Rejected or qualified claims

- **“Required and optional are two functions of a PP.”** Not in the current
  model: both are `adverbial`; requiredness records verb selection.
- **“Removal is the only way through.”** Too strong. Removal tests whether a
  particular verb frame survives. It cannot identify a PP's form or distinguish
  every possible function.
- **“The current five pairs are the whole lesson.”** They are strong evidence
  for a narrow contrast but omit the reverse mapping from one function to many
  forms and most PP functions.
- **“A PP's job can be read from the preposition or its final position.”**
  False. Those observations can help find the words, but the containing
  structure establishes the relationship.
