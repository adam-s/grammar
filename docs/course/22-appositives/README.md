## What the lesson decides

| Decision          | In plain words                                                           |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `func:appositive` | a neighbouring noun phrase that gives another way to identify a referent |

An appositive is a relation, not a punctuation mark or a kind of noun. Two
adjacent noun phrases are in apposition when the context makes them pick out the
same person or thing: _the captain_ and _a Scot_ in _The captain, a Scot,
resigned_. The second phrase has the `appositive` function. It is part of the
larger noun phrase that fills the clause's subject or object slot; it does not
fill a second clause slot of its own.

The relation is to the whole anchor phrase, not just to its head word. _A Scot_
renames _the captain_, determiner included. The bare proper names _Mermaid_ and
_Arun_ are still noun phrases for this purpose. By contrast, _New York_ is one
multiword name in the course model: it has `flat`, not `appositive`, function.
The learner must find two phrase boundaries before deciding that two nearby
nouns are apposed.

The course calls both close and comma-set-off cases `appositive`. **Integrated**
apposition is read as part of identifying the referent: _our guide Arun_ picks
out which guide. **Supplementary** apposition adds a second description of an
already identified referent: _our guide, Arun,_ supplies the guide's name. The
terms _restrictive_ and _nonrestrictive_ are common teaching names for this
contrast, but _integrated_ and _supplementary_ keep the focus on how the phrase
is attached and interpreted. They are readings of the same course function, not
new labels to introduce in the builder.

## The central generalization

An appositive is a neighbouring noun phrase that shares the anchor phrase's
reference. An integrated appositive helps identify the referent; a supplementary
one adds information about a referent already identified. Commas in writing,
and a separate intonation group in speech, are evidence for the supplementary
reading; neither creates the appositive relation.

This leads naturally from lesson 21. A postmodifier narrows the head from inside
that noun phrase; an appositive supplies another noun phrase for the same
referent. Lesson 23 then returns inside a single noun phrase to show that form
does not by itself settle a word's function. This lesson must preserve that
distinction: apposition concerns the relationship between two phrases, not just
the form of the words beside each other.

## Evidence in the current course

| Example                                             | What it establishes                                                                                | Limit                                                                              |
| --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _The captain, a Scot, resigned._ (`fix-appositive`) | Two noun phrases share a referent. The appositive lies inside the subject noun phrase.             | It is only a supplementary case.                                                   |
| _Our guide Arun waved twice._                       | The name can be integrated and have no commas.                                                     | Context must make several guides plausible for the identifying reading to matter.  |
| _Our guide, Arun, waved twice._                     | The same words with commas present the name as added information about a guide already identified. | Punctuation records this reading in writing; it does not prove the relation alone. |
| _The court questioned the surgeon, a stranger._     | An appositive can be inside an object noun phrase, not only a subject.                             | The end position gets only one comma because the sentence ends there.              |
| _Lena, our new captain, explained the route._       | A name can be the anchor and a fuller description can be appositive.                               | It still follows its anchor; the set has no reversed phrase-order example.         |
| _New York_ (`fix-determinative-and-name`)           | Two adjacent noun words can be one name rather than two co-referring noun phrases.                 | This is a phrase-boundary contrast, not a punctuation contrast.                    |

The current page's main fixture is sound, but its caption must not say that the
appositive is "not part of the sentence frame" without qualification. The whole
noun phrase is the subject or object in the frame; the appositive is a dependent
inside that phrase and has no independent subject, object, or complement
function.

## Diagnostics and their limits

Use converging evidence rather than one classroom test.

1. Mark the two candidate noun phrases. Check that the second is not a noun
   premodifier or part of one proper name; `flat` is the course's contrast for
   the latter.
2. Ask whether both phrases identify the same person or thing in this context.
   _The captain_ and _a Scot_ do; _the path_ and _beyond the gate_ do not, and
   the latter is not a noun phrase.
3. Ask whether the first phrase already identifies its referent. If it does,
   the second phrase may be supplementary; if it needs the second to pick one
   referent from several, the reading is integrated. Commas and pause intonation
   support the former reading.

**Omission is evidence only for the right question.** Removing a supplementary
appositive often leaves a grammatical clause with the same anchor reference:
_The captain resigned_. Removing _Arun_ from _our guide Arun_ also leaves a
grammatical clause, but it removes the information that selected one guide.
Grammatical survival therefore does not distinguish apposition from every other
kind of modifier, and it cannot define supplementary status.

**A _be_ paraphrase is a check, not a definition.** _The captain is a Scot_
makes the shared-reference reading easy to notice, but it changes an appositive
noun phrase into a clause-level predication. It is helpful for the fixture and
connects to lesson 13's complement test, yet it cannot decide the tree: an
object complement fills a verb-licensed clause slot, while an appositive remains
inside the subject or object noun phrase. Indefinites also make the paraphrase a
class-membership claim rather than literal identity.

**Reversal is optional evidence.** _A Scot, the captain, resigned_ is possible,
but a reversed order can sound marked and change what the writer presents first.
It should not be the lesson's procedure or a required property of every
appositive.

## Common summaries and what they omit

| Common summary                     | What it gets right                                                         | What the lesson must add                                                                                            |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “An appositive renames a noun.”    | The shared-reference relation.                                             | It is normally a second noun phrase; it renames the full anchor phrase, and phrase boundaries matter.               |
| “Put commas around an appositive.” | Commas commonly mark a supplementary appositive in writing.                | An integrated appositive may have none. Punctuation follows the intended reading.                                   |
| “You can remove an appositive.”    | A supplementary appositive is often removable without breaking the clause. | Removal may change which person or thing is meant, and many non-appositives are removable too.                      |
| “Appositives explain a noun.”      | A second phrase can add a description.                                     | A description that merely modifies a head, such as a postmodifier, is not thereby another co-referring noun phrase. |

## Practice and fixture audit

The ten live sentences are materially better than the retired corpus: `c22-e`
and `c22-f` hold the words constant while the punctuation and reference claim
change. Nine sentences have comma evidence; one is integrated without commas.
Six use an indefinite _a_ phrase as the appositive, three use a bare proper

subject noun phrase and three in an object noun phrase.

The remaining shortcuts are real:

All ten practice appositives follow their anchor, and none reverses the phrase
order. That is an acceptable limit for practice, but the page must not make
post-anchor order part of the definition. Sentence 8 also sits after
_appointed_, a verb associated with lesson 13's object-complement frame; its
diagram or surrounding explanation must show that _a newcomer_ is within the
object noun phrase, not a second verb-licensed complement.

## Revision implications

1. Keep the direct answer's shared-reference claim, but replace “not part of
   the sentence frame” with the NP-in-a-frame relationship above.
2. Make the _Our guide Arun_ / _Our guide, Arun,_ pair the revealing contrast.
   It needs approved, non-graded fixture diagrams so the lesson does not reveal
   a practice answer. State the changed reference claim beside the punctuation.
3. Recast the current three tests as the phrase-boundary and shared-reference
   procedure. Present omission and the _be_ paraphrase as limited supporting
   evidence; remove reversal from the core procedure.
4. Retain `fix-determinative-and-name` as the strongest live confusion check.
   Explain `flat` in the course's plain terms before showing the formal label.
5. Use a subject fixture for the main diagram and one object example for
   breadth. If sentence 8 remains, contrast its object NP with an actual
   object-complement structure so the lesson does not reuse the same paraphrase
   for two different trees.
6. Do not add _supplementary_ or _integrated_ as builder labels at lesson 22.
   They help the prose explain a reading and prepare lesson 39's claim that
   punctuation is evidence, not definition.

## Rejected or qualified claims

- **“Commas define appositives.”** Rejected. They are written evidence for one
  reading; `c22-e` is the direct counterexample.
- **“Either phrase can be removed, so both are equal.”** Qualified. The result
  is often useful in a simple supplementary example, but it neither fixes
  phrase boundaries nor preserves reference in an integrated example.
- **“An appositive is just a postmodifier.”** Rejected. A postmodifier narrows a
  head; apposition relates two co-referring noun phrases.
- **“A _be_ paraphrase makes it an appositive.”** Rejected. The paraphrase
  supports a shared-reference reading but changes the structural level and can
  also describe an object-complement relation.
