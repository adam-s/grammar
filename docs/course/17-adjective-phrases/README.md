## What the lesson decides

Nothing new enters the label menu here. `AdjP` arrived in lesson 10, and
`premodifier` in lesson 16. This lesson's job is to make the **boundary** of an
adjective phrase visible: a phrase can be just its adjective head, or it can
include material that modifies or completes that adjective.

## Central generalization

An adjective phrase is headed by an adjective. The head can stand alone, take a
degree modifier before it, or take a complement after it; the complete phrase,
not just its last adjective, can work as a modifier of a noun or as a
predicative complement in a clause.

That is a relationship, not a word-count rule. _Cold_ is an adjective phrase in
_The lake water felt cold_. So are _unusually calm_ and _proud of her garden_.
The adjective is the head in all three, even though the other two contain an
adverb and a prepositional phrase.

The course's tree makes that analysis explicit: `AdjP` has `Adj/head`, with an
`Adv/premodifier` or `PP/complement` under it. Cambridge's learner grammar calls
the earlier material a modifier or premodifier and says an adjective phrase
always has an adjective head. A formal syntax source instead treats _very_ and
_extremely_ as degree-category modifiers of an adjective. Those are different
ways to model the same local relationship. This course should teach its `AdjP`
analysis as its own diagramming choice, not as the only terminology used in
grammar.

## The grammatical relationships

| Relationship         | What makes it true                                                                                                                                                           | Live or approved example                                                                                    |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Headedness           | The adjective supplies the phrase's category. The phrase may be one word.                                                                                                    | _cold_ in _The lake water felt cold_ (`c17-a`); _salty_ in `fix-vlink`                                      |
| Degree modification  | A degree word changes the scale of a gradable adjective; it modifies the adjective rather than the clause verb.                                                              | _unusually calm_ (`c17-b`), _dangerously narrow_ (`c17-d`), _slightly sour_ (`c17-f`)                       |
| Adjective complement | A phrase selected or licensed by the adjective remains inside the adjective phrase, even though it follows the adjective.                                                    | _proud of her garden_ (`c17-i`); _proud of it_ in `fix-adjective-complement`                                |
| Predicative function | An adjective phrase after a linking verb can predicate a property of the subject. An adjective phrase can also predicate a property of an object after the appropriate verb. | _felt cold_, _seemed proud of her garden_; _found the driver entirely blameless_ (`c17-j`)                  |
| Attributive function | An adjective phrase can modify a noun, usually before it, but a complement can force the whole phrase or the complement after the noun.                                      | _a very slow voice_; _the person responsible for security_ (Cambridge examples; no lesson 17 practice case) |

The complement/modifier distinction needs care. A degree word such as _quite_
in _quite anxious_ adjusts how anxious someone is; removing it normally leaves
a smaller adjective phrase. A complement can supply the relation the adjective
calls for in the intended meaning: _proud of her garden_ says what the pride is
about. The phrase _proud_ can also occur alone, so grammatical omission is not
a universal complement test. Removing _of her garden_ changes the claim, but
does not by itself prove that every occurrence of _proud_ requires an _of_
phrase. The course's `complement` label is a useful analysis of this particular
reading, not a claim that _proud_ is always incomplete without one.

Nor are all dependents before the head. Cambridge gives _strong enough_ as a
postmodifier and _too heavy to lift_ as a degree construction with a following
clausal complement. Lesson 34 already has the latter construction; lesson 17's
_too heavy_ (`c17-c`) stops before the clause.

## Examples and evidence

The current page has the right basic contrast: `fix-vlink` shows a bare `AdjP`
in _The soup tasted salty_, while `fix-adjective-complement` shows _proud of
it_ as a single `AdjP`. The second fixture is especially useful because the
linking verb takes the complete phrase as its subject complement.

The practice set now has ten sentences, not the former ten copies of one
shape:

| Shape                       | Sentences                | What it establishes                                                                                                    |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Bare adjective phrase       | `c17-a`                  | The phrase need not contain more than its head.                                                                        |
| Degree modifier + adjective | `c17-b`–`c17-h`, `c17-j` | A degree word can sit inside an `AdjP`; the phrase occurs with several linking verbs and once as an object complement. |
| Adjective + PP complement   | `c17-i`                  | A following PP can belong to the adjective phrase.                                                                     |

The table in [sentences.md](sentences.md) misdescribes _Those visitors were
quite anxious_ as having “a complement after the adjective.” Its live parse is
`Adv/premodifier + Adj/head`; there is no following complement. The dossier
should not repeat that error. This research pass does not change the sentence
page.

## Diagnostics and their limits

| Evidence a learner can use                                              | What it supports here                                                                                | Where it stops working                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Replace the whole run: _She seemed proud of it_ → _She seemed pleased_. | The complete run can occupy the same subject-complement slot.                                        | Substitution shows that a span can act as a unit in this construction; it does not define adjective phrases in every position.                                                                          |
| Try to replace only the adjective: _\*She seemed pleased of it_.        | _of it_ does not independently remain after a new adjective, which supports putting it with _proud_. | This depends on the replacement adjective and its licensing pattern. It is evidence for this phrase boundary, not a mechanical test for every PP after an adjective.                                    |
| Ask “how adjective?”: _How calm?_ → _unusually calm_.                   | A degree word is modifying the adjective, not the linking verb.                                      | It works with gradable adjectives. It is not a test for all modifiers, and _-ly_ spelling does not make a word a degree modifier.                                                                       |
| Remove a degree word: _unusually calm_ → _calm_.                        | The remaining adjective is still an adjective phrase; the earlier word was an internal modifier.     | Omission changes degree and may change the intended meaning. It does not show that all removable material is a modifier or that all non-removable material is a complement.                             |
| Look at position: before a noun versus after a linking verb.            | The same phrase form can be attributive or predicative.                                              | Position is a clue to function, not a definition of adjective or adjective phrase. Some adjectives are restricted to one position, and complement-bearing adjective phrases often occur after the noun. |

## Common summaries, and what they omit

| Common summary                                        | What it gets right                                     | What it leaves out                                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “An adjective phrase is an adjective plus an adverb.” | _very cold_ is a real adjective phrase.                | A bare adjective is also an adjective phrase; complements and postmodifiers can occur too.                                                                       |
| “An adjective describes a noun.”                      | It captures a common attributive use.                  | Adjective phrases also occur predicatively after linking verbs and as object complements; the phrase may describe a subject or object rather than modify a noun. |
| “Adjectives come before nouns.”                       | That is the usual attributive position.                | Predicative and postnominal adjective phrases are ordinary English, and some adjectives are position-restricted.                                                 |
| “The word after an adjective is a modifier.”          | Some following material does add optional information. | A selected PP or clause can be a complement inside the adjective phrase; attachment and meaning decide the analysis.                                             |
| “The head is the important word.”                     | It points toward headedness.                           | It does not say what the head controls: the phrase's category and the dependency of its modifiers and complements.                                               |

## Corpus audit and shortcut register

The live set fixes the old all-degree-modifier pattern, but its composition is
still uneven. Nine of ten examples are predicative, eight are a degree word
plus adjective, and only one contains an adjective complement. None is an
attributive multi-word adjective phrase, a postmodifier such as _enough_, or a
clausal complement. That is suitable for a small progression, but it cannot
alone support a broad claim about every adjective phrase.

| Shortcut                                                   | Why the current set invites it                                            | What would defeat it                                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “An adjective phrase has two words.”                       | Eight practice sentences have the degree-plus-adjective shape.            | `c17-a` and `fix-vlink` already supply a bare adjective phrase; make that floor case prominent.                                                             |
| “The _-ly_ word modifies the verb.”                        | Several degree words end in _-ly_ and most sentences use linking verbs.   | Compare _The road became dangerously narrow_ with a clause adverbial; show that _dangerously_ answers the degree of _narrow_.                               |
| “Everything after the linking verb is one-word adjective.” | Nine examples follow a linking verb, and most end at the adjective.       | `c17-i` and `fix-adjective-complement` show a PP inside the phrase.                                                                                         |
| “Adjective phrases only come after linking verbs.”         | The set has nine subject complements and one object complement.           | An approved attributive phrase with internal material is missing from lesson 17.                                                                            |
| “The last adjective is the whole phrase.”                  | That works on the degree examples if the learner ignores the premodifier. | The whole-phrase replacement works on _proud of it_; a purpose-built degree fixture could make the same boundary visible without exposing practice answers. |

## Revision implications

1. **Keep the present central contrast, but say the whole claim.** The existing
   bare-_salty_ versus _proud of it_ contrast proves the lower and right-hand
   boundaries. The opening should name a degree modifier too, because eight
   practice sentences use that shape.
2. **Use whole-phrase replacement as evidence, not a definition.** The page's
   _proud of it_ → _pleased_ contrast is strong. Its prose should avoid saying
   that every complement is syntactically impossible to omit; this one changes
   the intended relation and cannot be left stranded after the replacement.
3. **Add one separate, approved degree fixture before revising the page's
   visuals.** It should display `AdjP > Adv/premodifier + Adj/head` without
   revealing a graded practice answer. The current diagrams never make the
   dominant practice shape visible.
4. **Add an attributive phrase with internal material to the explanation or a
   future practice revision.** _A very slow voice_ would connect lesson 16's
   premodifier work to phrase headedness. If it has a complement, use a
   postnominal example such as _the person responsible for security_ rather
   than implying that every complete adjective phrase precedes the noun.
5. **Correct the `c17-h` sentence-table note in the revision pass.** It has a
   degree premodifier, not a complement. Do not change it during this research
   pass.
6. **Leave clausal complements to lesson 34, but connect them accurately.**
   Lesson 17 can mention that _too heavy to lift_ extends the same phrase; its
   own _too heavy_ is not evidence for that clause boundary.

## Sources opened and read

- Cambridge English Grammar Today, [“Adjective phrases: position”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/adjective-phrases-position): attributive and predicative distribution, degree modifiers, _enough_, and comparative and infinitival complements.
- Cambridge English Grammar Today, [“Adjective phrases: functions”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/adjective-phrases-functions): attributive, subject-complement, and object-complement functions; the placement of complement-bearing adjective phrases.
- Cambridge English Grammar Today, [“Subject complements”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/parts-of-speech): the distinction between subject complements and objects, and the phrase forms that can be subject complements.
- Edward Stabler, UCLA, [_The Course in Minimalist Grammars_, chapter 12](https://linguistics.ucla.edu/people/stabler/185-13.pdf), pp. 155–56: adjective phrases as left modifiers of noun phrases and degree expressions as modifiers of adjectives. This is a formal framework, included to record the framework difference rather than to supply learner terminology.

## Rejected

- **Treating the lesson as a lesson about degree adverbs.** Degree modifiers are
  useful evidence for the boundary, but the lesson's subject is the adjective
  phrase they modify. Lesson 18 takes up adverb phrases.
- **Defining adjective phrases by the word before or after the adjective.** A
  bare adjective phrase has neither. The head and its dependencies explain the
  structure.
- **Using omission as the definition of complement.** Meaning, selection, and
  phrase structure have to converge; simple grammaticality after deletion is
  not enough.
