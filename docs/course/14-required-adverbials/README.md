## Central generalization

A required adverbial fills a slot that the verb selects in this use; an optional
adverbial adds a circumstance to a clause that is already complete. The same
kind of information, in the same kind of phrase, can do either job. A location
does not become required merely because it answers _where?_, and a
prepositional phrase does not become optional merely because it is a
prepositional phrase.

In _She placed the box under the bench_, the destination is part of the
caused-placement relationship: someone put the box somewhere. In _She opened
the box under the bench_, the same location only sets the scene for a complete
opening event. This course calls the first a **required adverbial**. Other
grammars call the same kind of verb-selected constituent a **complement**. The
lesson must make that framework choice visible rather than presenting its label
as the only one in English grammar.

## The relationship to show

The contrast is about the verb phrase, not the phrase's internal form.

| Clause                                    | What the location relates to                                              | Course analysis                   |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| _The keys are on that table._             | A locative use of _be_ needs a setting to complete the neutral statement. | SVA; required adverbial           |
| _The driver put the engine at the depot._ | _put_ describes causing the engine to be at a destination.                | SVOA; object + required adverbial |
| _The driver waited at the depot._         | Waiting is already a complete event; the PP supplies its setting.         | SVO + optional adverbial          |
| _She opened the box under the bench._     | Opening is already a complete event; the PP supplies its setting.         | SVO + optional adverbial          |

The PP has a second relationship inside itself: in _under the bench_, _under_
heads the PP and _the bench_ is its complement. That internal complement is
not the same thing as the PP's clause-level job. The current page says this,
but needs to place it after the larger verb–adverbial relationship is clear.

## Evidence and its limits

### Removal, with the use held still

Remove the whole candidate adverbial and read the remaining clause as an
ordinary, out-of-context statement in the same verb sense.

- _She opened the box under the bench_ → _She opened the box._ The clause is
  complete, so the PP is optional in this use.
- _She placed the box under the bench_ → _\*She placed the box._ The ordinary
  placement sense lacks its destination, so the PP is required in this use.
- _The driver waited at the depot_ → _The driver waited._ The setting can go.
- _The driver put the engine at the depot_ → _\*The driver put the engine._ The
  caused-placement sense lacks a destination.

This is good evidence for a required slot; it is not a definition of every
complement. A shortened sentence may be less informative but still complete.
Conversation can also recover omitted material, and a verb may acquire another
sense when a phrase disappears. Do not count _She put the milk away_ as the
result of removing _in the fridge_: it is a different construction. For the
locative _be_ examples, _The keys are_ is incomplete as a neutral, standalone
location claim, but English permits short or elliptical uses of _be_ in the
right context. The lesson should ask whether the original frame survives, not
whether the remaining words can ever be understood.

### What does not decide it

The questions _where?_, _when?_, _how?_, and _why?_ can identify a broad
adverbial meaning, but they do not distinguish required from optional. Neither
does word order, nor whether the unit is a PP or an AdvP. Both members of
_placed/opened the box under the bench_ are final PPs that answer _where?_.
Only the first completes the verb's selected relationship.

## Common summaries and what they omit

| Summary                                            | Useful part                                                    | Missing limit                                                                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| “A required adverbial cannot be removed.”          | It gives a usable first test.                                  | The test must preserve the verb's intended sense and control for ellipsis and context.                                                           |
| “Adverbials tell how, when, where, or why.”        | It groups many circumstance meanings.                          | Meaning does not tell whether the verb selected the phrase.                                                                                      |
| “A PP after _be_ is an adverbial.”                 | It notices a common locative pattern.                          | A PP is a form, and grammars disagree whether a required locative is an adverbial or a complement.                                               |
| “Complements are required; adjuncts are optional.” | It captures the valency contrast used by many modern grammars. | Some complements are omissible, so successful removal is stronger evidence for a required element than for the full complement/adjunct analysis. |

The terminology is genuinely mixed. Quirk's SVA and SVOA tradition uses
**obligatory adverbial**; Huddleston and Pullum reserve **adjunct** for the
optional type and call the verb-selected unit a **complement**. The app may
keep its course label, provided learner copy says that it is describing a
required place in the verb's pattern.

## Current corpus: strengths, shortcuts, and gaps

The live practice set has seven required and three optional adverbials.

| Reading                       | Sentences           | What it prevents or exposes                                                              |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Required locative after _be_  | c14-a–c             | Shows the SVA pattern, including an indefinite subject.                                  |
| Optional adverbial            | c14-d, c14-e, c14-j | Prevents “every adverbial is required.” It includes both an AdvP and PPs.                |
| Required object-plus-location | c14-f–i             | Prevents “only _be_ needs a location.”                                                   |
| Matched contrast              | c14-i / c14-j       | Prevents “a final PP after an object is required.” Only _placed/opened_ changes.         |
| Nearer verb contrast          | c14-e / c14-f       | Shows that a similar place phrase can be optional with _waited_ and required with _put_. |

This is a substantial repair of the earlier corpus, which had ten required
adverbials, all with _be_. The present set now makes the removal procedure do
real work. It still has two deliberate limits:

- Every required item is locative and is either a locative _be_ clause or an
  object-plus-location clause. Do not generalize that required adverbials are
  always places or always follow an object.
- It has no confident no-object, non-_be_ required example. Reference grammars
  often list uses of _go, lead, hang, live,_ and _stand_, but their acceptability
  without a location shifts with sense and context. The current conservative
  set is better evidence for a first binary procedure than a disputed example
  would be.

The remaining shortcut is that the first three items all have _be_ and all are
required. A learner can succeed on that small run by pattern matching. The
later mixed pairs undo it, but the practice order should introduce an optional
PP sooner or interleave the _waited/put_ contrast earlier if the app permits.

## Revision implications

1. Replace “The difference is found by removal, not by meaning” with the
   central generalization above. Removal is evidence; verb-selected structure
   is the reason the evidence matters.
2. Keep the current removal procedure and its warning about useful information,
   but add its two limits: hold the verb sense and context still, and remove the
   whole phrase.
3. Make a purpose-built, approved contrast fixture that mirrors the
   _placed/opened … under the bench_ pair, or mark those practice sentences as
   demonstrated before exposing their full parses. The present main contrast,
   `fix-vbe` versus `fix-subject-phrase-moved`, changes too much at once.
4. Retain `fix-vbe` to show the PP's internal structure, but label the scope:
   the PP is a required adverbial **in this course's analysis**; other modern
   grammars call it a locative complement.
5. Keep `fix-main-verb-irregular` only as a short breadth example for an
   optional AdvP. It demonstrates form versus function, not the central
   required/optional decision, so it cannot carry the lesson's main diagram.
6. Keep the c14-i/c14-j pair and the c14-e/c14-f near-pair. They are the
   practice set's strongest protection against form, position, and _be_-only
   shortcuts. Do not add a no-object required example unless its verb sense and
   removal judgment can be defended in the dossier.

Lesson 13 establishes that a phrase's form does not identify its function, and
lesson 14 should make the same point with adverbials. Lesson 15 then needs its
procedure to state that the verb predicts a slot in a particular use; it should
not reduce this lesson to “remove the last phrase.”

## Rejected or qualified claims

- **“Every adverbial is optional.”** False: a verb's pattern can require a
  locative, directional, temporal, or manner-like element.
- **“Required means semantically important.”** Too loose: optional details can
  be vital to the message. The question is whether the intended verb frame is
  grammatically complete.
- **“The removal test defines a complement.”** Too broad: it reliably reveals a
  non-omissible required element, but an omissible complement is still possible
  in fuller grammatical analyses.
- **“The PP in a locative _be_ clause is universally an adverbial.”** A
  framework choice, not a settled label. The course's SVA label is defensible
  when it is named as such.
