## What the lesson decides

| Decision              | In plain words                                           |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| `func:indirectObject` | the first object in the course's double-object structure |
| `vt:Vg`               | this verb use licenses indirect and direct objects       |

The course calls the `Vg` frame **ditransitive**: the verb is followed directly
by two NP objects.

## The central generalization

**In the course's double-object construction, the verb licenses two separate NP
dependents. The indirect object comes first and the direct object comes
second.**

> _He gave **her** **the keys**._

The order is part of this construction. It does not solve the larger question
of whether every `V NP NP` sequence contains two objects. Lesson 13 introduces
the competing pattern:

> _They elected **the lawyer** **their chair**._

There, the first NP is the sole direct object and the second NP says what that
object became. The two clauses have the same surface sequence but different
relationships. “Take the first NP after the verb” works only after the
double-object frame has been established.

## Structure before participant labels

Indirect objects often denote recipients or beneficiaries:

- _gave **the class** homework_
- _bought **her** flowers_

That meaning is a useful prototype, not the definition. With _owe you money_,
_teach the squad a routine_, _wish Alex a happy birthday_, or _cost me ten
dollars_, the first object is not simply a person who receives a transferred
thing.

The structural claim is narrower and stronger: in this course's accepted `Vg`
analysis, both NPs are licensed by the verb, the first has indirect-object
function, and the second has direct-object function. Semantic roles vary across
verbs and constructions.

## The _to_ or _for_ alternation

Many English verbs allow a related prepositional construction:

> _He gave **her** **the keys**._
>
> _He gave **the keys** **to her**._

The double-object version has two NP objects. In the prepositional version, the
direct object remains an NP and the other participant appears inside a PP. The
course therefore does not label _her_ an indirect object in _to her_: it is the
complement of the preposition inside a different construction.

This alternation is strong evidence with verbs such as _give, hand, show,
bring,_ and _write_. It is not the defining test for every indirect object.
Lexical and semantic restrictions matter:

- _donate the books to the library_ does not normally alternate with *_donate
  the library the books_;
- _cost me ten dollars_ has no ordinary *_cost ten dollars to me_ counterpart;
- _envy her confidence_ does not preserve its construction as *_envy confidence
  to her_.

The choice between _to_ and _for_ is also not mechanical. _To_ commonly marks a
goal or recipient; _for_ commonly introduces a beneficiary. A _for_-PP can be
an optional adjunct rather than the alternate of a licensed indirect object.
The paraphrase should therefore be taught as evidence available for many verbs,
not as a universal conversion rule.

## What position proves, and what it does not

Within the standard double-object pattern, position distinguishes the two
objects:

```text
V     indirect object    direct object
gave  her                the keys
```

The current diagram caption says their order alone does not identify them. That
is misleading. Their order identifies their functions **once the construction
is known**. What order alone cannot establish is whether the two post-verbal NPs
form a double-object construction or an object-plus-complement construction.

The learner page should use the diagram to show two NP slots licensed by the
verb, then use the _to_ paraphrase and the lesson-13 contrast as evidence for
which construction is present.

## The available evidence

### Constituency

Each object is a separate NP. Pronoun replacement can confirm those boundaries:

> _The porter handed **the guest** **a key**._
>
> _The porter handed **her** **it**._

Naturalness and ordering vary when both objects are pronouns across varieties
of English. The important local evidence is that one pronoun does not replace
the whole two-object sequence; each NP independently fills a verb-licensed
slot.

### The prepositional alternant

For a verb that licenses both constructions, moving the first object after the
second with _to_ or _for_ preserves a closely related transfer or benefactive
relationship. This is the most useful lesson-12 procedure, provided its lexical
limit is stated beside it.

The learner copy's phrase “the thing that stays beside the verb's central
meaning” is not structural evidence. In the double-object construction, the
direct object is the second NP. In the prepositional alternant, it becomes the
NP immediately after the verb. The syntax, not conceptual closeness, explains
the change.

### Passive

English often allows the first object to become the subject of a passive:

> _The guest was handed a key._

Some varieties also allow a passive with the other object promoted. Passive
behavior is useful evidence that the double-object construction contains
object relations, but its judgments vary and the course does not teach passive
voice until lesson 37.

### The lesson-13 contrast

The strongest evidence is a controlled pair with the same visible shape:

> _They called her a taxi._
>
> _They called her a genius._

Only the first supports a benefactive paraphrase: _They called a taxi for her._
In the second, _a genius_ predicates a description of _her_. The pair forces the
learner to distinguish two objects from object plus object complement. Lesson
15, which mixes all six verb frames, is the natural place to make this choice
explicit.

## Common summaries and their limits

| Summary                                        | What it captures                 | What it misses                                                                |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “the receiver”                                 | the prototype with _give_        | non-transfer verbs and less concrete roles                                    |
| “answers to whom or for whom”                  | prompts a useful alternant       | overgenerates and confuses PP participants with NP indirect objects           |
| “the first noun after the verb”                | standard double-object order     | the first NP in an object-complement construction is a direct object          |
| “the object that can move after _to_ or _for_” | many alternating ditransitives   | verbs that allow only one construction and meaning changes between alternants |
| “a verb with three noun phrases”               | subject plus two post-verbal NPs | does not distinguish `Vg` from `Vc`                                           |

The author-level summary keeps construction and evidence separate:

> A double-object construction contains an indirect-object NP followed by a
> direct-object NP, both licensed by the verb. A related _to_ or _for_
> construction provides useful evidence for many verbs but is not available
> universally.

## Current corpus audit

The pre-conversion set made every indirect object definite and every direct
object begin with _a_. The live set repairs that shortcut:

- _The clerk showed **a visitor** **the map**_ reverses the article pattern;
- _He wrote **his sister** **the letter**_ uses two definite NPs;
- _The porter brought **the guests** **their cases**_ uses a possessive direct
  object;
- _The nurse brought a blanket_ and _The porter carried the heavy cases_ are
  one-object controls;
- _The guide gave a map to us_ turns the prepositional alternant into a separate
  parsed sentence.

Eight items use the double-object frame and two use an ordinary transitive
frame. The set now defeats “three NPs always means two objects,” “the NP with
_a_ is direct,” and “this verb spelling always has two objects.”

One analysis deserves later review: the course currently gives the _to us_ PP
in _The guide gave a map to us_ an `adverbial` function. Reference grammars
often treat this goal/recipient PP as a complement licensed by _give_, even
though its internal NP is not an indirect object. The lesson can contrast the
two surface constructions without resolving that later PP-function decision,
but the fixture and lesson 14 should be checked for consistency.

## What the research should change

1. **State the order accurately.** In the established double-object
   construction, indirect object precedes direct object; order alone cannot
   decide whether the clause is double-object or object-complement.
2. **Present _to/for_ as a common alternation, not the definition.** Name lexical
   restrictions and avoid claiming every `Vg` use converts.
3. **Remove “closest to the verb's central meaning.”** It supplies no observable
   structural relation.
4. **Connect the method to the lesson-13 competitor.** The learner needs to know
   what the paraphrase rejects, not merely how it transforms an obvious _give_
   sentence.
5. **Keep the varied live practice set.** It now blocks the determiner shortcut
   and includes one-object controls.
6. **Audit the _to us_ PP analysis.** Decide with lesson 14 whether the course
   calls a verb-selected recipient PP a complement or an adverbial.

## Rejected

- **“The indirect object is the receiver.”** It is a prototype, not the
  grammatical relation.
- **The _to/for_ paraphrase as a defining conversion.** Not every verb licenses
  both constructions.
- **Position as a test before the frame is known.** `V NP NP` also describes an
  object-complement construction.
- **Article choice as evidence.** The former corpus accidentally made it a
  perfect shortcut; the live set correctly removes it.
- **Conceptual closeness to the verb.** It cannot be observed or applied
  consistently.
