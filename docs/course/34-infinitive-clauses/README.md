## Central generalization

An **infinitival clause** is a subordinate clause whose predicate is built on a
plain-form verb and does not carry the sentence's primary tense or agreement.
In the course's live examples, infinitival _to_ marks that form; it does not
head the predicate or decide what job the whole clause does.

That last distinction is the lesson. _To renew the lease_ is a direct-object
clause after _wanted_; _to lift_ completes _too heavy_. Both have the same
infinitival form, but they occupy different places in the larger structure.

The course uses `Part` plus `marker` for infinitival _to_ and records
`fin:infinitival` on the clause. This is a model choice, not a claim that all
grammars give _to_ the same category: the Cambridge grammar calls _to_ a
subordinator that marks the head VP. What both analyses make clear is that _to_
is not a verbal particle and is not the verb's head.

## Form, function, and subjects

### To-infinitival and bare infinitival forms

The familiar form is _to_ plus a plain verb: _to renew_. It is not the only
infinitival form. English also has **bare infinitivals**: _can leave_, _made us
wait_, and _let the dog sleep_. The ten practice sentences and all live lesson
34 diagrams use only the first form. A learner may use _to_ to find the
course's examples, but the page must not define every infinitive as “_to_ plus
a verb” or imply that every plain verb after another verb is finite.

The course can accurately say that its current target is the **to-infinitival
clause**. It cannot yet teach a contrast with a bare infinitival clause because
there is no approved bare-infinitive fixture or practice sentence.

### Marking is not the predicate

In _She wanted to leave the engine_, _wanted_ carries the matrix clause's past
tense. _Leave_ heads the embedded predicate; it can take _the engine_ as its
object. _To_ introduces or marks that embedded clause. The spelling comparison
with _She walked to the engine_ is useful because prepositional _to_ takes an
NP complement, while infinitival _to_ occurs with a plain verb. It is a
diagnostic for these two uses of the spelling, not a definition of all clauses
or all particles.

The course's naming needs this qualification: it labels _to_ a particle because
the palette's `Part` category has a distinct infinitival subtype. Traditional
and reference grammars may call it an infinitival marker or subordinator. Do
not make the label disagreement itself a learner problem; teach the observable
relation instead.

### Understood and overt subjects

In _The tenant wanted to renew the lease_, the tenant is naturally understood
as the renewer. The lower clause has no pronounced subject. This is a
subject-control-like pattern, but a lesson should not name it as a universal
rule. Other verbs allow an overt lower subject: _They asked the driver to
wait_; an infinitival clause can also have a _for_ subject, as in
_for anyone to lift_. Raising and control analyses distinguish several such
patterns, and whether an understood subject is supplied by a matrix subject,
matrix object, context, or raising predicate depends on the selecting verb.

The app can draw the overt subject in `fix-two-markers`, but it has no field for
the semantic link between that lower subject and another NP. It must not label
_the driver_ as an object complement in _asked the driver to wait_: an object
complement describes or renames the object; _to wait_ does neither. Until the
model records the relevant relationship, keep control and raising out of the
scored analysis and state this as a scope limit.

### Complements and modifiers

An infinitival clause may be a complement of a verb, adjective, noun, or whole
construction; it may also modify a noun or express purpose. Form does not settle
that job. The current material establishes verb complements and the adjective
complement in _too heavy to lift_. It has no noun-modifying infinitive (_a book
to read_) and no purpose infinitive (_stopped to rest_). Those must not appear
in a general statement as if the practice had demonstrated them.

The final practice sentence deserves special care. In its fixture and revised
live constructor, _to lift_ has the comparative clause kind and a direct-object
gap. That keeps `c34-j` aligned with the approved `fix-hollow-clause` analysis
while placing the clause as an adjective complement.

## Diagnostics and their limits

| Procedure                                                                             | Evidence it gives                                                                           | Limit                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Find _to_ followed by a plain verb; compare _to renew_ with _to the bank_.            | Distinguishes the infinitival marker from prepositional _to_ in this contrast.              | It misses bare infinitivals and a split infinitive such as _to carefully renew_. Spelling alone never settles the whole clause's function. |
| Move the matrix sentence in time: _The tenant wants to renew_ / _wanted to renew_.    | _Wants/wanted_ changes while _renew_ remains plain, so the embedded predicate is nonfinite. | Participial and gerund-participial clauses are also nonfinite; the test does not distinguish all nonfinite forms.                          |
| Ask what the lower verb takes: _renew the lease_, _wait outside_, _fund the repairs_. | Shows that the infinitival clause contains its own predicate and dependents.                | It does not identify the outer function of the whole clause.                                                                               |
| Replace a whole complement with _it_: _The tenant wanted it_.                         | Supports the direct-object analysis of `c34-a`–`c34-i`.                                     | It does not apply to `c34-j`, and pronoun replacement does not define what an infinitival clause is.                                       |
| Read _The box was too heavy to lift_ with the lower verb's missing object.            | Supports the external link between _box_ and the thing lifted.                              | It is a hollow/comparative construction, not a general diagnostic for every infinitival clause.                                            |

Constituency is best taught through layered evidence, not through a deletion
trick. The lower verb and its object or adverbial form a predicate; marker plus
predicate form the embedded clause; then the whole clause fills an outer slot.
Coordination or substitution can support that analysis in a suitable sentence,
but neither proves a category on its own.

## Teaching summaries to keep or qualify

| Common summary                                   | Keep                                                                           | Missing limit                                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| “An infinitive is _to_ plus a verb.”             | It recognizes the ten live to-infinitivals.                                    | Bare infinitivals exist, and a modifier can intervene between _to_ and the verb.                                                                                   |
| “An infinitive has no tense.”                    | Yes: the infinitival predicate does not carry primary tense or agreement.      | Nonfiniteness alone does not identify the form, and an embedded event can still be understood in time from context.                                                |
| “The infinitive is the object.”                  | It fits nine live examples.                                                    | `c34-j` is an adjective complement; infinitival clauses can have other functions.                                                                                  |
| “The main subject does the infinitive's action.” | It fits the nine direct-object examples well enough to be a first observation. | Object control, overt subjects, context-dependent readings, and raising defeat it.                                                                                 |
| “Never split an infinitive.”                     | Remove.                                                                        | A split infinitive has a modifier between _to_ and the verb; it changes neither clause boundary nor grammatical job. It is a style choice, not a parsing category. |

## Practice and coverage audit

The current ten sentences allow a learner to get the new labels right through
one surface pattern: every one has _to_ plus a plain verb, and the first nine
put that sequence immediately after a matrix verb. The learner does not have to
decide where the clause ends, distinguish its outer function, or account for a
lower subject.

`c34-h` and `c34-i` are the useful departures within the repeated pattern:
their lower adverbial and object show that the embedded predicate has its own
dependents. `c34-j` defeats “all infinitives are direct objects,” but its
_too … to_ frame is itself an easy surface cue and its live clause kind disagrees
with the fixture. There is no current exercise that tests the to/preposition
contrast, bare infinitival form, an overt infinitival subject, or a
noun-modifying/purpose infinitive.

## Revision direction

1. Lead with the form/function distinction: a nonfinite lower predicate may
   fill different outer slots. Use `fix-infinitive` as the main visual because
   it cleanly shows the marker, lower predicate, and two different objects.
2. Use `fix-hollow-clause` beside the final practice sentence to show a genuine
   contrast in outer function. The revised `c34-j` now matches the fixture's
   comparative clause kind.
3. Include the narrow _to renew_ / _to the bank_ contrast, then state that it
   identifies this course's to-infinitivals rather than all infinitives.
4. Add `fix-two-markers` only if the page needs a subject contrast. It gives an
   honest overt-subject example without falsely modelling object control. The
   visible explanation must say that _for_ and _to_ do separate jobs.
5. Replace at least one of the redundant direct-object practice sentences with
   the already approved _The box was too heavy for anyone to lift_ after its
   reading is accepted. It pairs the existing hollow clause with an overt lower
   subject. Do not add a scored control or raising example until the model can
   represent the dependency.
6. Either add a reviewed bare-infinitive fixture and practice contrast or call
   the page “to-infinitival clauses” in its opening scope. Never let the page's
   definition outrun its examples.
7. Keep split infinitives out of the main lesson. If learner copy names them at
   all, give one sentence: a modifier between _to_ and the verb does not create
   a new tree or a grammar error.

## Sources opened and read

- [Cambridge Dictionary, “Infinitives with and without to”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/infinitives-with-and-without-to).
  Read in full. It distinguishes to-infinitival and bare forms, lists bare
  infinitivals after modals, _let_, _make_, and optional _help_, and supplies
  examples with both an overt lower object and a subjectless to-infinitival
  clause. It supports the form claim, not the course's model labels.
- [Huddleston, Pullum, and Reynolds, _A Student's Introduction to English
  Grammar_, Chapter 14 overview](https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/books/a-students-introduction-to-english-grammar/EB0ABC6005935012E5270C8470B2B740/non-finite-clauses/8C8CCC879F04B8B2014746C2804A3CAA).
  The accessible chapter overview was read. It separates to- and bare
  infinitivals, says nonfinite clauses lack primary tense/agreement and often
  lack a subject, and explicitly notes overt subjects, inferred predicands, and
  raising. Its terminology differs from the app's `Part` label; that difference
  is recorded above rather than hidden.
- [Kim and Michaelis, “Raising and Control
  Constructions”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/syntactic-constructions-in-english/raising-and-control-constructions/CFE3ADD342DC072BCB91B5B34B7F3CAC).
  Read the publisher's chapter summary. It distinguishes a control predicate's
  coindexed unexpressed subject from raising predicates, which do not give the
  shared NP a semantic role. This supports the lesson's decision not to turn
  “same subject” into a rule.
- [Merriam-Webster, “split infinitive”](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/split%20infinitive)
  and [its usage note](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/to-boldly-split-infinitives).
  Read in full. They define the construction as a modifier between _to_ and the
  verb, document its long history, and treat it as broadly accepted when it aids
  clarity. This settles the scope question: it is usage advice, not a syntactic
  label.

## Rejected as lesson content

- **A ban on split infinitives.** It changes neither the clause's form nor its
  function.
- **A control/raising taxonomy.** It is real grammar, but the current model
  cannot encode the relation the taxonomy names.
- **A general lesson on bare infinitivals.** The research requires the limit,
  but the course currently has no approved example that can teach it.
