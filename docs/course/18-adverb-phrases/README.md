## What the lesson decides

Nothing new enters the label menu here. `Adv`, `AdvP`, `adverbial`, and
`premodifier` have already appeared. This lesson makes three separate things
visible: an adverb is a word class; an adverb phrase is a phrase headed by an
adverb; and that phrase can do different jobs depending on what it attaches to.

## Central generalization

An adverb phrase is headed by an adverb. It can be just that one head word, as
in _late_, or it can include words that modify the head, as in _very quietly_.
The complete phrase can modify a verb or another part of the sentence; the
adverb inside it can instead modify an adjective or another adverb.

That is why word count and _-ly_ spelling do not define the phrase. In _She
waited very quietly_, _quietly_ is the adverb head and _very_ depends on it, so
the whole `AdvP` is the clause's adverbial. In _The road was surprisingly
narrow_, _surprisingly_ is still an adverb, but it modifies _narrow_ inside an
`AdjP`; it is not the clause's adverbial. Form, phrase boundary, and function
are related questions, not three names for the same thing.

Cambridge's learner grammar calls the adverb the head of an adverb phrase and
allows material before or after that head. The course draws _very quietly_ as
`AdvP > Adv/premodifier + Adv/head`. That is a compatible diagramming choice,
not a reason to say that every grammar uses the course's exact labels for the
dependents.

## The grammatical relationships

| Relationship                                | What makes it true                                                                                                                                        | Live or approved example                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Headedness                                  | The adverb supplies the phrase's category. A one-word `AdvP` is still a phrase, and extra material belongs to it only when it depends on its adverb head. | _late_ in _The train arrived late_ (`c18-b`); _quietly_ in `fix-adverb-phrase`                       |
| Degree modification inside an adverb phrase | A degree adverb changes the degree of the head adverb. The degree word is not an independent modifier of the verb.                                        | _very quietly_ (`fix-adverb-phrase`); _unusually early_ (`c18-f`); _remarkably loudly_ (`c18-g`)     |
| External function                           | The whole phrase can modify the event or predicate. The course calls that external function `adverbial`.                                                  | _very quietly_ modifies _waited_; _twice_ modifies _checked_ (`c18-h`)                               |
| Scope and attachment                        | The same word class can attach at different levels. Its target determines the phrase it belongs to and the job it has.                                    | _remarkably_ modifies _loudly_ in `c18-g`; _surprisingly_ modifies _narrow_ in the `AdjP` of `c18-j` |
| Position                                    | An adverb phrase may occur at the front, in the middle, or at the end of a clause. Position is a distributional fact, not the phrase's definition.        | _Yesterday_ (`c18-c`), _quietly_ (`c18-d`), and _calmly_ (`c18-i`)                                   |

The phrase/function distinction needs to stay explicit. _Remarkably loudly_
and _surprisingly narrow_ each contain an `Adv` followed by the word it
modifies, but only the first complete phrase is an `AdvP`. The second complete
phrase is an `AdjP`, because its head is _narrow_. Calling both first words
“adverbials” would hide that attachment difference.

The familiar labels _manner_, _time_, _place_, _frequency_, and _degree_ group
adverbs by meaning. They do not replace phrase form or attachment. _Late_,
_yesterday_, and _twice_ give different kinds of information but have the same
course structure as `AdvP/adverbial`. Degree is different in the live examples:
_very_, _unusually_, _remarkably_, and _surprisingly_ are `Adv/premodifier`
inside the phrase they scale. The optional companion lesson can discuss the
meaning labels without promoting them to syntax labels.

## Examples and evidence

`fix-adverb-phrase` is the right central figure. It shows the two boundaries
the current page needs: _very_ is inside the `AdvP` with its head _quietly_, and
the `AdvP` is the adverbial of _waited_. It does not reveal a graded practice
answer.

The ten practice sentences now provide a small but useful spread:

| Shape                                | Sentences                         | What it establishes                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| One-word `AdvP` used as an adverbial | `c18-a`–`c18-e`, `c18-h`, `c18-i` | A phrase need not be longer than its head; it can occur after an intransitive verb, after an object, and in more than one clause position. |
| Fronted and medial `AdvP`            | `c18-c`, `c18-d`                  | An adverb phrase is not defined by being clause-final.                                                                                     |
| Degree modifier + adverb head        | `c18-f`, `c18-g`                  | A two-word `AdvP` can have an internal `Adv/premodifier` and `Adv/head`.                                                                   |
| Adverb inside an adjective phrase    | `c18-j`                           | An `Adv` can modify an adjective without becoming the clause's adverbial.                                                                  |

The set makes _-ly_ a poor shortcut on purpose: _late_, _yesterday_,
_overnight_, _early_, and _twice_ do not end that way. It also has the useful
contrast _remarkably loudly_ / _surprisingly narrow_, though the present page
does not yet make that contrast visible.

## Diagnostics and their limits

| Evidence a learner can use                                                                                               | What it supports here                                                                                            | Where it stops working                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Keep the adverb head and remove a degree word: _very quietly_ → _quietly_.                                               | _Quietly_ remains an `AdvP`; _very_ was a dependent that set its degree.                                         | The meaning changes. Deletion does not prove that every removable word is a modifier, nor that every non-removable word is a complement.                    |
| Move the whole natural phrase: _She waited very quietly_ → _Very quietly, she waited_.                                   | In this sentence, both words can occupy the front position together, which supports treating them as one phrase. | Fronting is not available or equally natural for every adverb phrase. It is evidence about this boundary, not the definition of an adverb phrase.           |
| Try to move the degree word by itself: _\*Very, she waited quietly_.                                                     | _Very_ cannot take the external adverbial position on its own here; it depends on _quietly_.                     | A bad isolated movement can reflect meaning, information structure, or an individual word's distribution. It does not by itself establish phrase structure. |
| Ask what the adverb changes: _How narrow?_ → _surprisingly narrow_; _How loudly?_ → _remarkably loudly_.                 | The answer distinguishes an adverb attached to an adjective from one attached to an adverb.                      | This is an interpretation-and-scope check, not a mechanical question test. A learner must first understand the sentence's intended meaning.                 |
| Compare positions: _Yesterday the children played_, _The children quietly left_, _The auditor checked the ledger twice_. | The same phrase form can be fronted, medial, or final.                                                           | Position helps rule out the false “last word” rule, but it does not identify every adverb or distinguish adverbials from other optional material.           |
| Notice _-ly_ spelling.                                                                                                   | It is a clue for many manner and degree adverbs.                                                                 | It misses many adverbs and falsely catches adjectives such as _friendly_ and _lively_. It cannot identify a phrase boundary or its attachment.              |

Omission belongs beside lesson 14's limit. _She waited_ remains a sentence
after the whole `AdvP` is removed, so that particular phrase is optional in
that particular frame. The result supports its adverbial function there; it
does not define adverbs, prove the phrase boundary, or license deletion of an
adverbial a verb requires.

## Common summaries, and what they omit

| Common summary                           | What it gets right                                    | What it leaves out                                                                                                                                                            |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “An adverb tells how, when, or where.”   | Many adverbials supply that kind of information.      | It turns semantic classes into a definition, misses frequency, degree, stance, and linking uses, and says nothing about phrase structure or attachment.                       |
| “An adverb phrase is two or more words.” | _Very quietly_ is an adverb phrase.                   | A bare adverb head can itself be an `AdvP`; phrasehood is headedness, not a minimum word count.                                                                               |
| “Adverbs end in _-ly_.”                  | Many manner adverbs do.                               | _Late_, _early_, _yesterday_, _overnight_, and _twice_ are counterexamples; some _-ly_ words are adjectives.                                                                  |
| “An adverb modifies a verb.”             | That is the common external use in this practice set. | An adverb can modify an adjective, another adverb, a noun phrase, a prepositional phrase, a clause, or a sentence. _Surprisingly narrow_ already defeats the verb-only claim. |
| “An adverbial is an adverb.”             | Many `AdvP`s are adverbials.                          | `adverb` is a word class, while `adverbial` is a function. A future form/function lesson must show that other phrase forms can be adverbials too.                             |

## Corpus audit and shortcut register

The current live set corrects the old corpus gaps. It has nine `AdvP`s used as
clause adverbials: seven one-word phrases and two phrases with a degree
premodifier. One is fronted, one is medial, and seven are final. The tenth
sentence supplies a different attachment: an `Adv/premodifier` inside an
`AdjP`, not an external `AdvP/adverbial`.

It remains a deliberately narrow sample. There is no place adverb, no
postmodified adverb phrase such as _quickly enough_, no sentence adverb, no
adverb phrase with an auxiliary in its middle position, and no required
adverbial. Those omissions are acceptable if the page narrows its claims to
headedness, degree modification, scope, and the positions the corpus actually
shows.

| Shortcut                                                 | Why the current set invites it                                 | What defeats it                                                                                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “The adverb phrase is the last word.”                    | Seven of nine external `AdvP`s are clause-final.               | `c18-c` fronts _Yesterday_; `c18-d` places _quietly_ between subject and verb.                                                           |
| “An adverb phrase must have two words.”                  | The title and main fixture foreground a multi-word phrase.     | The seven one-word `AdvP`s, especially _late_ in `c18-b`, make the head-only floor case explicit.                                        |
| “Every adverb ends in _-ly_.”                            | Several prominent examples do.                                 | _late_, _yesterday_, _overnight_, _early_, and _twice_ require the learner to use more than spelling.                                    |
| “Every adverb modifies the verb.”                        | Nine sentences contain an external `AdvP/adverbial`.           | `c18-j` places _surprisingly_ inside an `AdjP`, modifying _narrow_.                                                                      |
| “The word before an adverb is another clause adverbial.” | _unusually early_ and _remarkably loudly_ contain two adverbs. | In those examples, the first adverb scales the head adverb; the whole two-word `AdvP`, not the first word alone, modifies the predicate. |

## Revision implications

1. **Keep `fix-adverb-phrase` as the main figure, but state the hierarchy.**
   The current caption is sound; the prose should name the `Adv` head, its
   degree dependent, and the complete `AdvP`'s external function. “The two
   words travel as one unit” is a useful observation for this sentence, not a
   definition to generalize without its limit.
2. **Make scope the lesson's revealing contrast.** Show the approved fixture
   _very quietly_ beside a sentence or diagram that shows _surprisingly narrow_.
   The same word class has different targets, so one complete phrase is an
   `AdvP` and the other an `AdjP`. Do not expose a graded practice sentence as a
   completed answer; build an approved fixture if the page needs a diagram.
3. **Keep movement and omission as qualified evidence.** The current procedure
   should say that fronting the whole phrase supports its boundary in this
   example, while removal supports optionality in this verb frame. Link the
   latter limit back to lesson 14 rather than presenting either operation as a
   universal test.
4. **Use the live position range in prose or a non-graded visual.** The page's
   main fixture is clause-final, so it cannot by itself defeat the strongest
   old shortcut. A fronted and a medial example should appear without turning
   the page into a parade of semantic types.
5. **Do not teach the traditional semantic list as this lesson's analysis.**
   It is useful background for the optional companion, but manner, time, and
   frequency do not change the tree in the live examples. Preserve the model's
   deliberate distinction between semantic classes and structural labels.
6. **Leave form/function breadth to lesson 20, but prepare it accurately.**
   This page may say that `adverbial` names a job and that the examples happen
   to use `AdvP`s. It should not claim that all adverbials are adverbs or
   adverb phrases.

## Sources opened and read

- Cambridge English Grammar Today, [“Adverb phrases”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/adverb-phrases): adverb phrases can be one word or have premodifiers and postmodifiers; the adverb is their head; phrase meanings and functions extend beyond verb modification.
- Cambridge English Grammar Today, [“Word classes and phrase classes”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/phrase): a word class supplies the head of its phrase, and phrases can have dependents before or after the head.
- Cambridge English Grammar Today, [“Adverbs and adverb phrases: position”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/adverbs-and-adverb-phrases-position): front, mid, and end positions; the limits and preferences associated with semantic groups and clause structure.
- Cambridge English Grammar Today, [“Adverbs: forms”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/adverbs-forms): the limited value of _-ly_, adverbs not derived from adjectives, and degree modification of gradable adverbs.
- Cambridge English Grammar Today, [“Adverbs: functions”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/adverbs-functions): adverbs can modify verbs, adjectives, adverbs, noun phrases, prepositional phrases, clauses, and sentences.

## Rejected

- **Defining an adverb phrase as a multi-word expression.** A head alone makes
  a phrase; the live set now proves both the one-word and internally modified
  cases.
- **Using fronting or omission as a universal constituent test.** Both are
  useful evidence in the controlled fixture, but acceptability depends on the
  construction, the word, and the intended meaning.
- **Equating the semantic kinds of adverb with syntactic labels.** They answer
  a meaning question. The course diagrams attachment, phrase form, and
  function.
- **Teaching `_ly_` as a rule for finding adverbs.** It is a weak spelling clue
  and cannot identify a phrase boundary or distinguish an adverb from every
  adjective ending in `_ly_`.
